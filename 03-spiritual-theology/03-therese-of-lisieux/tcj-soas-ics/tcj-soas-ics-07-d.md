# Reflections and Questions – Chapter VII. The First Years in Carmel

## Reflection 1

We Kissed At The Barrier; and Passing Through

She left me, and moment by moment got

Smaller and smaller, until to my view

She was but a spot;

A wee white spot of muslin fluff

That down the diminishing platform bore

Through hustling crowds of gentle and tough

To the carriage door.

Under the lamplight’s fitful glowers,

Behind dark groups from far and near,

Whose interests were apart from ours,

She would disappear,

Then show again, till I ceased to see

That flexible form, that nebulous white;

And she who was more than my life to me

Had vanished quite...[^4]

This scene is repeated countless times each day at airports, railway stations, bus terminals, and ocean piers. But no two partings are the same, for no two intimacies are identical. Yet every human heart that beats knows the truth of Emily Dickinson’s words; “Parting is all we know of heaven and all we need of hell.”[^5]

Thérèse knew the hell of parting at a tender age. “Casting a last look upon Les Buissonnets, that beautiful cradle of my childhood, which I was never to see again, I left on my dear king’s arm” (S 230). In addition to the pain that is part and parcel of every parting, there were two factors that increased the pain of Thérèse’s separation from her family. The first was that there was a certain finality about her parting—“casting a _last_ look... _never_ to see again” (S 230). It is onething to say _Adieu_, knowing that a separation is temporary; it is a far different reality to say _farewell forever_. There was a certain forevermore quality that attended upon Thérèse’s departure. While it is true that Thérèse would be able to see her family after she entered Carmel, the thousand and one ways of being and doing that bound them together, were gone forever. The experience of Thérèse’s loss was similar to that of the death of a loved one. We do not know how much we are connected to people whom we deeply love until they are taken from us. This is because they are so much a part of our lives, so linked to every memory triggered by the senses, that everything around us is a reminder of that person’s absence. “But O for the touch of a vanish’d hand / And the sound of a voice that is still! /... But the tender grace of a day that is dead/Will never come back to me.”[^6]

The second factor that added pain to Thérèse’s departure was that it was the result of _her choice_. Thérèse could have stayed home with her father for the rest of his life, or at least until she was twenty-one, as had been recommended to her, but she chose not to. In consequence, Thérèse had to bear a suffering almost too heavy to bear. Namely, watching someone you deeply love, suffer, knowing that you are the cause of the suffering and that you could have prevented it. The town gossip that seeped through the cloister walls was that Louis had lost his mind because his daughters, most particularly his youngest, had abandoned him in his old age. Had not Louis exclaimed, “Oh, my children are abandoning me!”[^7] This vicious rumor was tactlessly talked about by the nuns in Thérèse’s presence.

Place yourself in Thérèse’s situation. You have made a decision to do God’s will, which will entail leaving your elderly father, knowing full well that your choice will cause him to suffer greatly. Two months after your departure, you receive news from home that your father has become disorientated. You hear rumors that it was yourchoice to leave home that was the catalyst of his breakdown. Finally, you are a scrupulous person who is prone to rumination and self doubt. Imagine the guilt that you would feel, and the second guessing as you call into question the choice that you have made. So, what do you do? What is God’s will for you in this situation? There are no easy answers to this question. But let us presume that in spite of your doubts, you believe that God wants you to stay where you are. In such a situation, there is a two-fold suffering that you are called to bear. First, holding fast to a choice that is besieged by a barrage of doubts. Second, like Mary at the foot of the cross, you have to watch someone you love suffer and feel helpless to do anything about it.

## Question 1

What choices in your life entail these kinds of sufferings?

## Reflection 2

“Peace is my farewell to you, my peace is my gift to you. I do not give it as the world gives peace” ([[john-14#^john-14-27|Jn 14:27]]). Jesus said these words on the most anxious night of his life, the night before he died. The peace that Jesus is referring to is a peace that can coexist with pain and anxiety; it is the peace of soul that ensues upon doing the right thing, or knowing that you have done your best or have not left anything undone. It is the peace of a clear conscience.

Thérèse writes of this peace, regarding her first years in Carmel: “For seven years and a half that inner peace has remained my lot... in the midst of the greatest trials” (S 230). Also, when Thérèse was devastated because she had not received permission to enter Carmel from Leo XIII, she retained this peace of soul. “In the bottom of my heart I felt a great peace, since I had done everything in my power to answer what God was asking of me. This _peace_, however, was in the _depths_ only; bitterness _filled_ my soul, for Jesus was silent” (S 206). In addition, on the day that Thérèse received the veil (September 24, 1890), and her father was unable to attend the ceremony, Thérèse still was able to retain the peace of God. “Everything was sadness andbitterness. And still _peace_, always _peace_, reigned at the bottom of the chalice” (S 262). The basis of Thérèse’s peace was that she accepted suffering as a part of God’s will. “I see very clearly that it’s God’s will that he not be here... Today’s trial is a difficult sorrow to understand... [but] let us accept with a good heart the thorn that Jesus is offering us” (L 684). The three long years that Thérèse’s father was confined to the mental asylum in Caen were the most painful of her life; yet, she could write that they were “the most fruitful of my life” (S 241), for she was given the grace to see all things as a part of God’s mysterious design.

## Question 2

Think of times in your life when you made a painful decision to do the will of God and felt the strength of God’s peace. Conversely, there is a false peace that “brings perpetual war” writes St. Teresa.[^8] It is the temporary relief that we experience in the short run, that follows in the wake of a choice that _avoids_ doing God’s will, but which in the long run, leaves a queasy feeling in the pits of our stomachs because we know that we are living in an uneasy alliance with our conscience. Have you ever found yourself in this state of soul?

## Reflection 3

One morning, several years ago, I was sitting at my desk working on a retreat conference, when I heard a knock at my door. It was one of my Carmelite brothers who needed a ride to the airport. I said, “Yes,” to his request but was tense until I was able to get back to my desk. I remember saying to myself, “Well, that shoots the morning.” As I heard myself saying these familiar words, I was struck by their strangeness. Why would an act of charity shoot my morning? Isn’t growing in love what life is all about? Yes, but the purpose of life often gets in the way of my agenda! Langston Hughes’ question, “What happens to a dream deferred?” may be personalized as “What do you do when your plans are frustrated? What do you do when your life is put onhold?” How do you interpret interruptions in your life? Henri Nouwen once told the story of a research professor who was always complaining about interruptions _in_ his work, until one day he came to the realization that interruptions _were_ his work. Have you ever felt that your life was put on hold? The next time that you feel this way, ask yourself this question, “Whose life am I living now, if not my own?” Even though _our plans_ are often frustrated by unseen roadblocks and _our agendas_ laid waste by a world that hasn’t yet come to consciousness of the supreme importance of the designs that we have upon our day, it is impossible for _our life_ to be put on hold, for whatever is happening in the moment is our life. As John Lennon puts it, “Life is what is happening to you as you’re making other plans.”

Thérèse’s plans were often frustrated. The dates of her entrance into Carmel, her reception of the habit and her profession, were all postponed. One of the dangers in such situations is that we can subtly disengage from life or slacken our efforts to do God’s will, until our _real_ life begins. Thérèse experienced this temptation when her entrance date into Carmel was postponed. “How did those _three_ _months_ pass, those months so rich in graces for me? At first the thought came into my mind not to lead a life as well regulated as had been my custom” (S 215). But then Thérèse realized the importance of the present moment. “But soon I understood the value of the time I was being offered. I made a resolution to give myself up more than ever to a _serious_ and _mortified_ life.... My mortifications consisted in breaking my will, always so ready to impose itself on others, in holding back a reply, in rendering little services without any recognition, in not leaning my back against a support when seated, etc., etc... I cannot express how much this waiting left me with sweet memories” (S 215–16).

## Question 3

During the various times when Thérèse’s plans were frustrated, she did not put her life on hold, but rather embraced these interruptions as opportunities to grow in virtue. In your daily life, where are you faced with similar opportunities?
