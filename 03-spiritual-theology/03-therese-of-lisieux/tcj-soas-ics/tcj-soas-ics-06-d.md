# Reflections and Questions – Chapter VI. The Trip to Rome

## Reflection 1

In Spite of Thérèse’s Shyness and The Fact That The Majority of The pilgrims were from the upper echelon of French society, Thérèse found herself able to enter into conversation with them. “We very quickly became acquainted with the different people on the pilgrimage. So timid that I usually dared not to speak, I was surprised to find myself completely freed from this crippling fault” (S 193). This “fault” may best be seen as an expression of Thérèse’s personality change that took place as a result of her mother’s death. “My happy disposition completely changed after Mama’s death. I, once so full of life, became timid and retiring... [and] could not bear the company of strangers” (S 49). We may attribute the overcoming of this “fault” to her Christmas conversion. Nevertheless, we should not conclude that this newfound freedom made it _easy_ for Thérèse to enter into conversation with strangers, for the effects of the deep wounds of our childhood are never completely eradicated. Thérèse discovered that her crippling anxiety to talk to strangers had disappeared, but her choice to enter into conversation with them still required effort on her part.

## Question 1

Some of us are like Thérèse; we do not feel at ease in a group of strangers, and when we find ourselves in such a gathering, our natural tendency is to remain silent and let everyone else do the talking. However, there are times when God calls us to step out of the circle of our shyness or reserve and speak a truth that needs to be heard. Conversely, some of us have the tendency to take over or dominate a conversation and find it difficult to be quiet and to listen. Whatever the case may be, like Thérèse, God often asks us to “step out of character” for the sake of others. If you are a shy person, can you think of times when God asked you to overcome your natural timidity for the sake others? Conversely, if your ego tends to fill a room at social gatherings, can you remember a time that God whispered in your ear to remain silent for the sake of others?

## Reflection 2

Bernard MacLaverty’s “The Beginnings of Sin” is a story of the shattering of innocence. Column, a fatherless boy from a poor Irish family, so idolizes Father Lynch that he is unable to see that he is a frail human being. When Miss Grant, the parish housekeeper had to announce to the early morning congregation, with increasing regularity, that “Father Lynch was ill and that there would be no Mass that day,” it never even crossed Colum’s mind to doubt that Father Lynch was ill.[^3] However, “Column [who often served Father Lynch’s Mass] always wondered why Father Lynch was so nervous saying his morning Mass. He had served for others and they didn’t tremble like that.” Colum’s innocence and idealization of Father Lynch lead him to interpret the trembling in the best light.” Perhaps it was because [Father Lynch] was holier than [other priests], that they weren’t as much in awe of the Blessed Sacrament as he was.”[^4] Column looked up to Father Lynch as the father he didn’t have, and to make Father Lynch proud of him, he worked tirelessly to sell raffle tickets for the parish building fund. One night, after selling forty-two books of tickets, Column ran excitedly to the rectory to tell Father Lynch. Father Lynch was drunk. He staggered toward Column, tripped and lunged forward; his hand caught the side of Colum’s face, knocked off his glasses, and shattered them on the floor. Column left the rectory in a state of fear and confusion. He told his mother that he had broken his glasses when he slipped on the ice. The next morning, when Column entered the sacristy, he was surprised to see that Father Lynch was already there. After Mass, Father Lynch sat down and talked to Column about the previous night.

Father Lynch put his hand in his pocket and gave Column some folded pound notes. “Did you mention it to your mother?” “What?” “How they were broken?” “No.”“Are you sure? To anyone?” Column nodded that he hadn’t. He was turning to get out the door. The priest raised his voice, trying to keep him there. “I knew your father well, Column,” he shouted, “You remind me of him a lot.” The altar-boy ran, slamming the door after him. He heard an empty wooden coat-hanger rattle on the hardboard panel of the door and it rattled in his mind until he reached the bottom of the hill. There he stopped running. He unfolded the wad of pound notes still in his hand and counted one-two-threefour of them with growing disbelief.”[^5]

Colum’s disbelief was the blasting and shattering of innocence. Embarrassment, disillusionment, bewilderment, fear, shame, a sense of betrayal were all crumpled up in a bribe of secrecy in a fist full of one pound notes. What is shattered when innocence is betrayed at a tender age is hard to put into words. One’s universe is shaken because belief in the existence of goodness that is strong and loving is called into question. The loss of innocence is a fearful grief; it is the loss of a person whom we trusted, believed in, and looked up to; it is the lonesomeness that asks “whom can I go to now; whom can I trust; is there anyone who can understand my hopes and fears?” What makes the shattering of innocence in youth so devastating is youth’s inability to understand evil and process one’s feelings. But even to sophisticated, worldly-wise adults, the betrayal of innocence is still a heavy blow to bear. Psychiatrist Peter Rutter relates how devastated he was when he learned that his trusted and revered mentor was having sexual relations with his clients. To Rutter, his mentor represented all the best qualities of teacher and healer, a role model to be emulated. For a long time, Rutter denied the rumors about his mentor because “I still needed to protect my image of the ‘good father’”[^6]At the age of fifteen, Thérèse’s image of the “good father” was shattered. On her trip to Rome, Thérèse, who had always held priests in veneration and regarded them “as pure as crystal,” saw them for what they were—“weak and fragile men... [who] show in their conduct their extreme need for prayers” (S 190–91). This rude awakening neither scandalized nor dissuaded Thérèse from her vocation; rather, it answered a question that had always puzzled her: “Why did Carmel have a special mission to pray for priests?” Now she knew—they _needed_ prayers! Thus, Thérèse could write: “I understood _my vocation_ in _Italy_” (S 190).

## Question 2

How do we react when an icon is shattered? Do we lose faith in the human race? Do we become cynical or suspicious of goodness? Or like Thérèse, when we realize that our heroes and heroines are mere mortals, can we accept them as fellow human beings who need our prayers?

## Reflection 3

In John Cheever’s short story “Goodbye, My Brother,” four siblings and their spouses spend a week together at a seaside cottage, where the protagonist becomes increasing irritated at his brother Lawrence’s negativity. Lawrence constantly complains and criticizes everything and everyone. In a moment of rage, the protagonist picks up a branch and strikes Lawrence on the back of the head. Lawrence gets up, packs his bag, and goes home enraged and resentful. The story ends with the protagonist shaking his head and thinking to himself. “Oh, what can you do with a man like that? What can you do? How can you dissuade his eye in a crowd from seeking out the cheek with acne, the infirm hand; how can you teach him to respond to the estimable greatness of the race, the harsh surface beauty of life; how can you put his finger for him on the obdurate truths before which fear and horror are powerless?”[^7] At the end of the story we sympathize with the protagonist; yet, he is just as blind and obdurate as his brother Lawrence, for he tells us indirectly that his anger that culminated in violence has been a life long pattern. “I realized that our dislikes are as deeply ingrained as our better passions, and I remember that once, twenty-five years ago, when I hit Lawrence on the head with a rock, he had picked himself up and gone directly to our father to complain.”[^8] After a lifetime of conflict with his brother, he is still asking the same question, “Oh, what can you do with a man like that?” He is trapped in his futile response to Lawrence because he lacks the wisdom to accept the things he cannot change. So, what do you do with a man like Lawrence? Thérèse offers us one approach. On the train to Rome, there was a man similar to Lawrence. His “complaints were coming from his mouth constantly; he was unhappy with the carriages, the hotels, the people, the cities, everything” (S 196). What good would it have done to say to the man, “Stop your griping, you’re making everyone miserable?” In doing so, one would quickly become sucked into a row. Sometimes the best, the most prudent, the most charitable thing that we can do is to give these people a wide berth. But when we _have to_ deal with such people, we need to give up the futile goal of trying to talk them out of their negativity (or to beat it out of them, as the protagonist of Cheever’s story tried to do with his brother Lawrence), and simply love them as best we can, without expecting them to change. This is what Thérèse’s father did. “Papa, with his habitual kindness, tried to console him by offering him his place, etc.; he himself felt at home everywhere, being of a temperament directly opposite that of his disobliging neighbor” (S 196).

Louis Martin remained at home with himself because he did not lose himself by losing his composure. He _responded_ in love instead of _reacting_ in rage. His kindness did not _transform_ the man but it prevented Louis from being _deformed_. As the old adage cautions—be careful in choosing your enemies, for you will become a lot like them. At times, God calls us to be prophets, to confront and challenge the behavior of others; at other times, God calls us to be peacemakers, to accept people as they are. The later case, is neither giving up nor giving in, but letting go. It is the first step in accepting the things we cannot change.

There is a story of a man who tried every conceivable method to rid his lawn of dandelions, but failed. In desperation, he wrote to the Department of Agriculture. He gave an exhaustive list of the methods he had tried and asked what else he could do. He received the following reply. “Dear Sir, We have read your letter with great interest and have reviewed your various methods. We suggest that you learn to love your dandelions.”

## Question 3

What people are the “dandelions” in your life, that is, the people that God is asking you to love _as they are?_ Or, are you like Lawrence in Cheever’s story or the malcontent on the train to Rome —a person who complains about everything?
