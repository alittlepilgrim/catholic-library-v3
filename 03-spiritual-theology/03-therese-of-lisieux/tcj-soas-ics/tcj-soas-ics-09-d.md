# Reflections and Questions – Chapter IX My Vocation Is Love

## Reflection 1

After Marie read what Thérèse had written in her retreat notes, Marie experienced “a certain feeling of sadness” (L 997) for she did not recognize in herself the same burning desires of love that were evident in her sister. In response to Marie’s discouragement, Thérèse wrote the following: “How can you ask me if it is possible for you to love God as I love Him... If you had understood the story of my little bird, you would not have asked me this question. My desires of martyrdom _are nothing;_ they are not what gives me the unlimited confidence that I feel in my heart. They are, to tell the truth, the spiritual riches that _render one unjust,_ when one rests in them with complacence and when one believes they are _something great_... _These_ desires are a _consolation_... but when He does not give this consolation, it is a grace of _privilege._ Recall those words of Father: ‘The martyrs suffered with joy, and the King of Martyrs suffered with sadness.’ Dear Sister, how can you say after this that my desires are the sign of my love?... Oh, dear Sister, I beg you, understand your little girl, understand that to love Jesus... The weaker one is without desires or virtues, the more suited one is for the workings of this consuming and transforming Love” (L 998–99).

Like Marie, we can erroneously believe that our desires and fervor are the measure of our love for God; this is an example of judging our spiritual life with an emotional yardstick. Thérèse tells us that the opposite is true, namely, that our love for God is proved when we _act_ in a loving manner without the support of _affect_. In this regard, Thérèse writes to Céline: “What a grace when, in the morning, we feel no courage, no strength to practice virtue; it is the moment to put the axe to the root of the tree... we can dip into diamonds and what a profit at the end of the day... In one act of love, even _unfelt_ love, all is repaired, and Jesus smiles” (L 467). It is as if Thérèse is saying to Céline, “On those days when you get up in the morning and you don’t feel like praying or don’t have the energy to love your neighbor, isn’t it wonderful to know that these are the times that you can truly _prove_ your love for God, because it is precisely at these times that your love arises from your will alone without the support of emotion.”

## Question 1

What Thérèse calls _unfelt_ love is an invitation for us to look at situations in our lives from a spiritual perspective. For example, think of a person in your life whom you don’t like. How do you regard your feelings toward this person? Do you consider your feelings an obstacle to love? Do you feel guilty because you believe that you should not feel this way? Or, like Thérèse, can you view your feelings as an _opportunity_ to prove your love for God?

## Reflection 2

When Thérèse began meditating on 1 Corinthians 12, she found herself in _none_ of the various vocations that St. [[paul-saint#Index|Paul]] enumerates. “God has set up in the church first apostles, second prophets, third teachers, etc...” ([[1-corinthians-12#^1-corinthians-12-28|1 Cor 12:28]]), but as she read chapter 13, she found herself in _all_ of them; this made her exclaim, “Then, in the excess of my delirious joy, I cried out: O Jesus, my love... my _vocation_, at last I have found it... MY VOCATION IS LOVE!” (S 302) ^tcj-soas-ics-09-d-1

What exactly did Thérèse find? Some contend that it was her “little way.” But had Thérèse not found her “little way” years before during her encounter with Mother Geneviève? “God... willed that I live with a saint.... One that was made holy by the practice of the hidden virtues, the ordinary virtues.... Ah! That type of sanctity seems the _truest_ and the _most holy_ to me, and the type that I desire because in it one meets with no deceptions” (S 264–65). It is futile to try to pinpoint the _exact moment_ when Thérèse discovered her “little way” because her spirituality evolved over time; it would be better to say that toward the end of 1896, when Thérèse was meditating on 1 Corinthians 13, God enlightened her regarding a two-fold dilemma.

The first part of the dilemma was this: Thérèse believed that God does not inspire unrealizable desires. This conflicted with her powerlessness to fulfill the various desires that God had planted in her heart. “I feel the _vocation_ of the WARRIOR, THE PRIEST, THE APOSTLE, THE DOCTOR, THE MARTYR” (S 299). This dilemma was resolved when Thérèse realized that love was both the root of all of her desires and the bond that connects all the members of the Mystical Body of Christ. In consequence, whenever Thérèse loved, she _contributed to_ and _participated in_ all the works of the entire body. “If one member suffers all the members suffer with it; if one member is honored, all the members share its joy” ([[1-corinthians-12#^1-corinthians-12-26|1 Cor 12:26–27]]). “I shall be _Love_. Thus I shall be everything, and thus my dream will be realized” (S 302). In short, when we abide in love, we abide in God and in one another.

The second part of Thérèse’s dilemma is similar to the first. Thérèse desired to perform _extraordinary_ deeds for God. “I feel the need and the desire of carrying out the most heroic deeds for _Y ou, O_ _Jesus_,” (S 299) but Thérèse realized that she was “too little to perform great actions” (S 308). Her solution was the realization that “Jesus does not demand great actions from us but simply _surrender_ and _gratitude_... He has no need of our works but only of our _love_” (S 295).

Thus, what makes a deed extraordinary, is not the deed itself, but the _love_ that is contained in the deed, and Thérèse knew that all of her deeds, no matter how small, were done in love. “I have no other means of proving my love for you other than that of strewing flowers, that is, not allowing one little sacrifice to escape, not one look, one word, profiting by all the smallest things and doing them through love” (S 304–5).

Thus, it is because Thérèse’s _little sacrifices_ were true deeds of love that they were _extraordinary_ deeds. It is easy for us to miss the magnitude of this statement because love has been reduced to an emotion or trivialized to “being nice.” But love is neither of these watery realities; it is the deepest reality of life, for God is love. Thus when we love with the love of charity, our actions are the actions of God. St. John of the Cross writes that each action of a soul in union with God “is more meritorious and valuable than all the deeds a person may have performed in the whole of life without this transformation, however great they may have been... [because] all the acts of the soul are divine, since both the movement of these acts and their execution stem from God.”[^8] It is this mystical reality that is behind Thérèse’s words “I shall be _Love_” (S 302).

## Question 2

The same is true for us. Whenever we act with the love of charity, God loves through us and is made manifest in this world. Have you ever considered your “strewing of flowers,” your daily acts of charity, from this perspective?

## Reflection 3

“‘I have frequently noticed,’ Thérèse confided to me, [wrote Céline], ‘that after I have committed a fault, even a slight one, my soul experiences a certain sadness or uneasiness for some little time. Then I tell myself, ‘Now little one, this is the price you must pay for your fault, and so I patiently bear with the trial until the little debt is paid.’ According to Thérèse’s understanding of the virtue of hope, that was the extent of satisfaction required by Divine Justice for those who are humble and who abandon themselves to God in love. She could not believe such souls would have to go to Purgatory. She thought, rather, that at the moment of death their Father in heaven, because of their confidence, would kindle in their souls as they realized their misery an attitude of perfect contrition, and that in this way their entire debt should be cancelled.”[^9]

In short, Thérèse is saying, that by patiently bearing the pain that follows in the wake of committing some fault or failing, we are confiding ourselves to the mercy of God and it is by the mercy of God alone that we are purified. Thérèse gives us an example of how we are to confide ourselves to the mercy of God in her image of the “_weak little bird_,” who after committing “misdeeds, instead of going and hiding away in a corner, to weep over its misery and to die of sorrow... turns towards its beloved Sun, presenting its wet wings to its beneficent rays... recounts in detail all its infidelities, thinking in the boldness of its full trust that it will acquire in even greater fullness the love of _Him_ who came to call not the just but sinners” (S 307).

What Thérèse is proposing is more difficult than it seems, for when we are filled with guilt and a sense of our own wretchedness, it is hard to believe in the mercy of God, for guilt tends to project upon the face of God a stern grimace. Our sins do not alter God’s merciful love toward us; they simply make it more difficult for us to believe in it.

Julian of Norwich writes that there is no wrath in God. “God is that goodness which cannot be angry, for God is nothing but goodness.” Commenting upon this passage, Robert Llewelyn writes: “God’s face of compassionate love is always turned towards us, and if what we see in God is wrath, that is because of a corruption within ourselves which we have projected on to God, for our tendency is always to make God in our own image... Moreover we are not to be surprised, nor depressed, nor discouraged by occasions of falling, for what at least is certain is that we can never fall outside of God’s love. Our falling, Julian might well have said, is not so much a falling into sin as a falling into the arms of God’s mercy.”[^10]In Francis Thompson’s poem _The Hound of Heaven_, Thompson images God as a vicious hound that is pursuing him, but at the end of the poem when God finally corners him, Thompson realizes that he has been mistaken. What he interpreted as the menacing shadow of God’s wrath is revealed as the refreshing shade of God’s mercy; what he thought was God’s hand poised to strike him was in truth God’s hand reaching out to caress him. “Is my gloom, after all, Shade of His hand, outstretched caressingly?”[^11] Our sins can make us feel that we are beyond the reach of God’s mercy. Thérèse believed just the opposite. “I feel [that] even though I had on my conscience all the sins that can be committed, I would go, my heart broken with sorrow, and throw myself into Jesus’ arms, for I know how much He loves the prodigal child who returns to Him” (S 404).

The prodigal son returned home to his father’s house not for any noble motive but because he was hungry and down on his luck. Nevertheless, his father welcomed him home with open arms, without reproach. This is the image of God that Jesus reveals to us and we are invited to believe. But how difficult it is to believe in God’s mercy when we have squandered our inheritance and feel that we no longer deserve to be called God’s child. How difficult it is at these times not to give in to the temptation to run away and hide “in a corner, to weep over [one’s] misery and to die of sorrow”(S 307). What strength it takes to be like the little weak bird and present the wet wings of our soul to the Sun of God’s mercy.

## Question 3

Think of times when you have felt that your sins have put you beyond the reach of God’s mercy. During these times, what has helped you to believe in God’s merciful love?
