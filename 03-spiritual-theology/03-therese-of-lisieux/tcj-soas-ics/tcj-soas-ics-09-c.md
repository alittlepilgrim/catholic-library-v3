# Background and Context – Chapter IX My Vocation Is Love

In the spring of 1894, Thérèse began suffering from a persistent sore throat, a nagging cough, frequent fevers, and occasional chest pains. These early signs of Thérèse’s tuberculosis were not taken any more seriously than the early symptoms of Zélie’s breast cancer. Two years later, in April 1896, Thérèse suffered her first hemoptysis (spitting up of blood), a definite indication that she had contracted tuberculosis. In spite of this alarming symptom, Doctor de Cornière, the monastery’s official physician, refused to accept that Thérèse had tuberculosis and remained adamant in his opinion for the remainder of Thérèse’s life. In July 1896, after a cursory examination by Doctor de Cornière, Thérèse wrote the following to her aunt Céline: “Doctor de Cornière to whom I had the _distinguished honor_ of being presented yesterday in the speakroom... after having honored me with a look, declared that I looked well” (L 969)! And even as late as July 1897, three months before Thérèse’s death, he still insisted that she did not have tuberculosis. “It is not tuberculosis but rather an accident which has caused serious lung congestion.”[^1]

Pauline wanted Thérèse to be examined by Dr. Francis La Néele, the husband of her cousin Jeanne Guérin, but Mother Gonzague, who was a personal friend of Doctor de Cornière, dissuaded her.

Even though Pauline was the current prioress (up until March 1896, when Mother Gonzague was reelected), Pauline found it difficult to assert her authority in the face of Mother Gonzague’s wishes. “Yes it was Mother Marie de Gonzague who worked for my election, but she would only be hurt if I exercised too much authority. How I suffered and wept during those three years!”

Many in the community accepted the doctor’s diagnosis and believed that rest and the proper treatment would restore Thérèse to health. One of the reasons this belief could be maintained for so long was that Thérèse continued to work with the novices, was able to attend community functions, and hid her suffering from the community. But in the final months of her life, all of this changed. Thérèse stopped going to choir in April 1897, ceased attending community recreation the following month, and by June, no longer took her meals in the refectory.

As the tuberculosis was taking its toll, Thérèse was finding it increasingly more difficult to put up a good front. During one of the last times she attended community recreation, she sat next to Sister Marie of the Trinity and said to her, “I have come near you so that you can _guard_ me. I feel that I have not the strength to carry on a conversation. Look as if you are talking to me so that others won’t come to speak to me. I do feel so sick, but I don’t want to tell Mother Agnes yet, she would worry so much!”[^2]

On August 15, Thérèse began to experience great difficulty breathing. The next day, Mother Gonzague gave Pauline permission to contact Dr. Francis La Néele; Dr. de Cornière was away on vacation. After examining Thérèse, he wrote his findings. “The right lung is completely lost, filled with tubercles in the process of softening. The lower part of the left lung is affected.”[^3] After the examination, Dr. La Néele spoke harshly to Mother Gonzague. In consequence, Mother Gonzague “created a scene, lost her patience and railed against the whole [Martin] family, including the patient, but Thérèse remained calm,” testified Pauline.[^4]

It has been falsely alleged that one of the ways that Mother Gonzague railed against Thérèse was by refusing permission to administer morphine to Thérèse in her final agony. Céline says otherwise; it was not out of cruelty or revenge that Mother Gonzague refused to allow morphine to be given to Thérèse but because “sedatives were prohibited as a disgrace.”[^5] Likewise, even though “[Thérèse] was involved in some rather painful scenes caused by poor Mother Marie de Gonzague during Thérèse’s final illness,” it should not be concluded that there was anything vindictive in Mother Gonzague’s behavior, but rather that her behavior was an expression of her “unstable and eccentric temperament,” testified Céline.[^6]

By the end of the month, Thérèse’s intestines were gangrenous and she began to suffocate. The pain was so intense that Thérèse asked that all poisonous medicines be placed out of her reach for the fear that she would take her own life. Pauline testified: “She asked that we not leave poisonous medicines for external use within her reach and advised us never to leave them near patients suffering the same tortures, always for the same reason, the pain ‘is enough to make one lose one’s reason,’ and, no longer knowing what one is doing, one could easily take one’s life” (LC 162–63). “Yes! What a grace it is to have faith,” said Thérèse, “If I had not had any faith, I would have committed suicide without an instant’s hesitation” (LC 196).

In these final months of Thérèse’s life, there was no longer any question of either her diagnosis or prognosis; she had contracted tuberculosis and would die very soon. This was no surprise to Thérèse, for in April 1895, a whole year prior to her first hemoptysis, Thérèse confided to Sister Thérèse of St. Augustine, “I shall die soon,” (L 897). And on May 10, the following year, Thérèse had a dream in which Blessed Anne of Jesus told Thérèse that she would die very soon; the dream confirmed what she had known for a long time. Thérèse confided this presentiment of her death to her sister Marie. It is within this context, that Marie asked Thérèse, while Thérèse was making her personal retreat (September 7–18, 1896) to jot down a summary of her “little way of confidence and love.”[^7] Thérèse submitted to Marie’s request; it resulted in what would become chapter nine ([[tcj-soas-ics-09-a|Manuscript B]]) of _Story of a Soul_.Contained within this chapter are two important passages: first, Thérèse’s account of having discovered that love is the principle of unity that reconciled all of the various ways that she desired to show her love for God (“my vocation is love,” S 299–302); second, Thérèse’s story of “the little bird” (S 306–9) which is one of her best explanations of “the little way.”

Mother Gonzague’s decision to withhold painkillers from Thérèse should not be viewed as a masochistic act but rather as an expression of the spirituality of the times. Abbé André Combes writes: “The apostolic doctrine of the French Carmel is contained fully in the notion by which a Carmelite nun is put in the place of a sinner, acting as a substitute... to appease the justice of God by willingly accepting the punishments due to sinners. When this plan was taken literally, the fidelity of the nun to her calling was judged according to the intensity of her spiritual and bodily mortifications. Spiritual mortifications not readily admitting of measurement, much insistence was laid upon corporeal austerities. This was the ideal in favor at the Carmel of Lisieux under the government of Mere Marie de Gonzague. The cross of iron and the whip of nettles were held in high honor.” André Combes, _St. Thérèse and Her Mission_, trans. Alastair Guinan (New York: P. J. Kennedy and Sons, 1955), 134–35. It is also worth noting that in her final illness (cancer of the mouth) Mother Gonzague refused painkillers. She died on December 17, 1904.

An example of Mother Gonzague’s jealously that was exhibited during Thérèse’s final illness is recounted by Pauline. “On several occasions Thérèse was made to feel her jealousy which flared up because the novices often went to the infirmary. Just a few days before [Thérèse] died, Mother Marie de Gonzague spoke to her coldly and made her visits so brief that Thérèse asked one of the novices, ‘Do you know what I have done to upset Mother?’ ‘I know,’ replied the novice, ‘I did not wish to tell you lest I caused you pain. Our mother has noticed that I love you very much and that I am very happy when I can come and see you and she said to me: If I had known of your close ties with Sister Thérèse of the Child Jesus and how she suffices for you in everything, I would not have worried about you.’ Sister Thérèse of the Child Jesus replied simply to the novice: ‘Ah! That’s the reason. Thank you.’” Guy Gaucher, _Passion of Thérèse_, 111.

The secrets of Jesus to Thérèse are sweet, and I would like to hear about them once again. Write me a short note. This is perhaps your last retreat...” (L 991).
