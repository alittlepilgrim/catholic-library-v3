# Background and Context – Chapter VIII. Profession and Offering to Merciful Love

On August 28, 1890, Thérèse began a private retreat in preparation for her solemn profession. Like the retreat that she had made the previous year and those that she would make in subsequent years, this retreat was devoid of any spiritual consolation. Her prayer was arid and she felt abandoned.

Thérèse dreaded making retreats ever since her first communion retreat, which had triggered an attack of scruples. Retreats awakened painful memories in Thérèse and revived in her the fear of another attack of scruples.[^1] This fear was fed by many of the sermons that Thérèse heard in Carmel.[^2] Pauline testifies: “Scruples came to paralyze this impetus [trust in God], and she was very troubled, too, during her first years in Carmel. This was partly because she had heard it said in some sermons that it was very easy to offend God and to stain one’s purity of conscience.”[^3] This is exactly what Thérèse was told by Father Domin during her first communion retreat.

Thérèse’s fear of a recurrence of an attack of scruples was not an empty one, for on the eve of her profession, she began to have obsessive doubts regarding her vocation. “Not a single doubt concerning my vocation had ever entered my mind until then...

[The evening before my profession] my vocation appeared to me as a _dream_, a chimera.... The darkness was so great that I could see and understand one thing only; I didn’t have a vocation” (S 260–61).

Thérèse made her profession on September 8th, but she was still searching to find her path to God. The spirituality of the community, infected by [[jansenism#Index|Jansenism]], stressed appeasing the justice of a wrathful God by means of physical penances. Thérèse was not attracted by this stark spirituality. Thérèse’s God was a humane God. One example that shows that Thérèse’s God was a loving God rather than a wrathful God was her lack of fear and guilt over her habit of falling asleep during prayer. She writes that she did not feel “ desolate” about falling asleep during prayer because she “remembers that _little children_ are as pleasing to their parents when they are asleep as well as when they are wide awake” (S 259–60). ^tcj-soas-ics-08-c-1

Thérèse believed in a loving God. She knew that her path to God was _not_ the path of physical penances. But what her path would be, was still uncertain to her. She discovered her path when she was so depressed that she “no longer knew whether God loved [her]” (S 264). One day when Thérèse visited Mother Geneviève in the infirmary, she found two nuns with her. Thérèse excused herself. However, as she was leaving, Mother Geneviève said to her, “Serve God with _peace_ and _joy_; remember my child, _Our God is a God of_ _peace_” (S 264). These words were such healing balm to Thérèse’s beleaguered soul that she was convinced that Mother Geneviève had received a special revelation from God. It was in Mother Geneviève that Thérèse discovered a spirituality by which “one was made holy by the practice of the hidden virtues, the ordinary virtues... that type of sanctity seems the _truest_ and the _most holy_ to me” (S 265). A year after Thérèse’s profession, the community made its annual retreat (October 8–15, 1891) conducted by Father Alexis Prou. The retreat was a time of darkness for Thérèse. “I was having great interior trials of all kinds, even to the point of asking myself whether heaven really existed” (S 269). But just as Mother Geneviève was an unexpected ray of light that pierced Thérèse’s darkness the year before, so too was Father Alexis.

The community did not expect much from Father Alexis, who was assigned at the last moment to conduct the retreat, when his provincial Father Bénigene de Janville, who was slated to give it, had to cancel. Father Alexis had no experience giving retreats to religious; his ministry in the main was to large crowds of lay peoplein factories. At first, Thérèse, like the rest of the community, felt that Father Alexis was a bad fit. “I had made a preparatory novena with great fervor, in spite of the inner sentiment I had, for it seemed to me that the preacher would not be able to understand me since he was supposed to do good to great sinners but not to religious souls” (S 269).

In the light of this sentiment, coupled with her difficulty of expressing the trials that she was experiencing, Thérèse was “disposed to say nothing of [her] interior dispositions,” (S 269) to Father Alexis. Translated into the vernacular, Thérèse’s feeling about sharing her interior trials with Father Alexis was: “Why even try; he won’t be able to understand me anyway.”

“But I had hardly entered the confessional when I felt my soul expand. After speaking only a few words _I was understood_ in a marvelous way... He launched me full sail upon the waves of _confidence and love_ which so strongly attracted me... he told me that _my faults caused God no pain_... _This_ assurance filled me with joy... My nature was such that fear made me recoil; with _love_ not only did I advance, I actually _flew_” (S 269–70).

This account of Thérèse being freed from the crippling fear of scruples and being launched upon the waves of love was a great grace for her, but it can give us the erroneous impression that from this point on, Thérèse never had any further problems with scrupulosity. This was not the case. For example, nearly two years after this retreat, Thérèse became so scrupulous that she doubted that she was forgiven _after_ going to confession. In a letter dated January 20, 1893, Father Almire Pichon writes the following to Thérèse in this regard: “Dear Child of my soul, listen to what I am about to tell you in the name and on the part of our Lord: No, no, you have not committed any mortal sins. I swear it. No, we cannot sin gravely without knowing it. No, after absolution, we must not doubt about our state of grace. To your mother St. Teresa, who waspraying one day for souls who were deluding themselves, Our Lord answered: “My daughter, no one is lost without knowing it perfectly.” Banish, then, your worries. God wills it, and I command it. Take my word for it: Never, never, never, have you committed a mortal sin” (L 767).[^4]

In January 1892, influenza ravaged the community. It claimed the lives of three nuns and confined most of the others to bed. Only three nuns were left on their feet; Thérèse was one of them. She rose to the occasion by nursing the sick and preparing the dead for burial. She impressed and edified the community with her maturity and tireless dedication in the face of a difficult situation. The little girl with golden ringlets in her hair who was always taken care of, now took charge. Even Father Delatroëtte was swayed in his estimation of Thérèse. “She shows great promise to this community.”

After being devastated by influenza, the community received new life into its midst in the person of a Céline Martin, but not without opposition. Father Delatroëtte questioned whether the entrance of a fourth Martin girl was advisable or even permissible.[^5] Sister Aimée was adamant in her opposition and voted against Céline’s entrance. She felt that Céline would increase the influence of the “Martin clan,” as she called them. In addition, Sister Aimée, being of a practical bent of mind, felt that Céline, an “artist,” would be of little use to the community.[^6]

But in the end, Sister Aimée relented and Céline was received into the community on September 14, 1894. Céline entered when her sister Pauline (Mother Agnes) was prioress, having been elected to that position on February 20, 1893. Mother Gonzague was the novice mistress and Thérèse the assistant novice mistress. Céline was assigned to Thérèse’s guidance, which proved to be a task for both of them. While Céline was good-hearted and eager to learn, her independent spirit, impetuosity, and perfectionism made her a tall order for Thérèse, a salad “well seasoned with vinegar and spices” (S 387) as Thérèse referred to her.Even Céline recognized these traits in herself: “I was far too eager about my assignments, and I seemed to be pushed by an irresistible drive to carry them out, meticulously, to an exaggerated stage of perfection. Consequently, whenever I did not achieve this, I experienced a sense of frustration.”[^7]

[^6]: Sister Aimée’s opposition to Céline’s entrance may be viewed in part as another example of a clash of cultures. Sister Aimée, who came from peasant stock, was irritated by the “bourgeois” ways of the Martins. Her practical bent, which once voiced opposition to planting rose trees in the cloister quadrangle because she felt that planting potatoes would be more useful, was in opposition to Céline’s artistic nature. “Carmel does not need artists,” she adamantly proclaimed, “it has far greater need of good infirmarians and laundresses!”

Céline, by contrast, was highly educated. She was a voracious reader with a wide range of interests including Plato, religious and literary authors, and the scientific journals of her day. She was an accomplished artist and photographer; she had professional training in both areas.

[^7]: Geneviève of the Holy Face (Céline Martin), _My Sister_, 100. Thérèse’s counsel to Céline was as follows: “You have not come to Carmel merely to turn out a mountain of work or even, strictly speaking to become an indefatigable worker. Are you unduly concerned at the present moment as to what is happening in other Carmelite Monasteries, and whether the nuns there are busy or not? Does their work interfere with your prayer and meditation? Well, in the same way you should become detached from your daily occupations, conscientiously devoting to them the time prescribed but with perfect freedom of heart.”
