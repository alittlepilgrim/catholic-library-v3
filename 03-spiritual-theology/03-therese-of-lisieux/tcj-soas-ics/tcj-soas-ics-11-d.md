# Reflections and Questions – Chapter XI. Those Whom You Have Given Me

## Reflection 1

In _The Sketch of Mount Carmel,_ St. John of the Cross drew two roads that do not lead to the summit of the Mount (union with God). One path is entitled _The Goods of Earth_, which consists of different categories of earthly objects that a soul can become attached to: possessions, joy, knowledge, consolation, rest. The other path entitled _The Goods of Heaven_, consists of various spiritual gifts that a soul can become attached to: glory, joy, knowledge, consolation, rest. It is John’s teaching that all of the goods of earth and heaven are meant to help us on the way to salvation, but they can become obstacles if we relate to them with possessiveness. This is true even of the most spiritual gifts. For example, John tells us that God will often shower a soul with sensible consolations in prayer for the sake of weaning it away from the things of the world. Thus, a consoling experience of God’s presence can be beneficial to our spiritual growth, but if a person becomes attached to consolation, then it becomes an obstacle on the path, for the person begins to seek _a_ _consoling experience_ and ceases to seek the God who granted the experience; the person is more concerned with receiving a _gift_ than serving the _giver_ of the gift. As John writes, “Some [people] let themselves be encumbered by the very consolations and favors God bestows on them for the sake of advancing, and they advance not at all.”[^4]

Thérèse begins chapter eleven with her thoughts on our relationship to the spiritual goods of knowledge, “... The goods which come directly from God, inspirations of the mind and heart, profound thoughts...” (S 373). Thérèse writes that God has given her the grace not to lay claim to spiritual insights that have been granted to her. This grace allowed Thérèse to remain silent when another sister acted “as a thief” by passing off Thérèse’s insights as her own.Put yourself in Thérèse’s situation. One day you tell a person a profound insight that you received in prayer. Six months later, you read an article written by this person that contains your insight, and you are not even mentioned in a footnote. How do you feel? Enraged? Betrayed? Hurt? So what should you do in this situation? Should you confront the person about the “theft?” Perhaps, but only if you have discerned that God is asking you to do so. Or maybe God is offering you the grace to be silent for the sake of transformation? Maybe your outrage has nothing to do with the “theft.” Maybe it has everything to do with your sense of ownership and your need to be recognized as an insightful person. The Hindu monk Baba Hari Daas, who had not spoken a single word for over thirty years because of a vow of silence, was once asked what was the most difficult thing about keeping his vow. He said that it was the impulse to intervene in a conversation for the sake of dispelling ignorance. But over time, as his compulsion subsided, it became clear to him that his desire to intervene was rooted in vanity, in his desire to impress others with his wisdom and to be the center of attention. Over time, the clamor of his ego subsided, and his soul experienced profound peace and tranquility. This is the same tranquility of spirit that Thérèse received: “the grace of not being any more attached to the goods of the mind and heart than to those of earth” (S 374).

## Question 1

We can never achieve true peace of mind and heart without quieting the clamor and tumult of our ego. So often, the issues that we are so quick to defend or take offense at too readily are indicative of either a wounded or inflated ego. The things that drive us crazy about people and the issues that make us see red consume an enormous amount of psychic energy and keep us in a state of constant turmoil and agitation. Choosing to remain silent when we are bristling inside is an important step on the path to peace. The more that something galls us, the greater the peace thatwill ensue when it is put to rest. There is much wisdom contained in the advice, “Give it a rest,” which is often said to us by family or friends when we are harping upon an issue. Is there something in your life that you need to “_give a rest?_”

## Reflection 2

“I do not hold in contempt beautiful thoughts which nourish the soul and unite it with God; but for a long time I have understood that we must not depend on them and even make perfection consist in receiving many spiritual lights. The most _beautiful thoughts_ are nothing without good works. It is true that others can draw profit from them if they humble themselves and show their gratitude to God for permitting them to share in the banquet of a soul whom He is pleased to enrich with His graces. But if this soul takes delight in her beautiful thoughts and says the prayer of the Pharisee, she is like a person dying of hunger at a well-filled table where all his guests are enjoying abundant food and at times cast a look of envy upon the person possessing so many good things” (S 374–75). In the above passage, Thérèse touches upon a basic law of the spiritual life that goes far beyond the specific example of which she writes, namely, that whenever we feed our ego, we starve our spirit in the process, for it is only the love of God, not the approval and applause of the world, that can nourish our spirits. We might compare the consequences of trying to impress people with our spiritual “wisdom” to ingesting coffee into our system. The immediate effect of drinking coffee is feeling stimulated, but since caffeine depletes our body of B vitamins, we become malnourished in the process, and when the stimulation wears off, we feel drained.

## Question 2

Thérèse’s tragic image of a person who is dying of hunger at the very table that he or she has set out before others is akin to the fate of King Midas who almost starved to death because he obtained his wish that everything he touch be turned into gold.Have you ever fulfilled your desire of being the center of attention and felt spiritually empty afterwards?

## Reflection 3

As St. [[paul-saint#Index|Paul]] was departing from Ephesus, he said to the Christian community gathered, “I know that as I speak these words that none of you among whom I went about preaching the kingdom will ever see my face again. Therefore I solemnly declare this day that I take the blame for no man’s conscience, I have never shrunk from announcing to you God’s design in its entirety” ([[acts-of-the-apostles-20#^acts-of-the-apostles-20-25|Acts 20:25–27]]). It is a sobering, if not frightening fact, that to one degree or another we bear responsibility for the consciences of others, that it is a duty in charity to speak unpopular truths and confront people about their wayward behavior when God calls us to do so. So often, however, when God calls us to admonish or reprove our neighbor, we shrink back in fear and find refuge in silence. ^tcj-soas-ics-11-d-1

As novice mistress, Thérèse knew this fear first hand, for she was often called upon to correct a novice about her behavior. She found this difficult because she feared that her novices might judge her to be too severe. Nevertheless, she did not allow her fear to stand in the way of doing her duty.[^5]

“Nothing escapes my eyes... I would prefer a thousand times to receive reproofs than to give them to others; however, I feel it is necessary that this be a suffering for me, for... I know very well that your little lambs find me severe... [but] in the depths of their hearts they feel that I love them with a real love, that I never will imitate ‘_the hireling who sees the wolf coming and leaves the_ _sheep and takes to flight_’” (S 380–81). Is it not true for most of us that we prefer to receive a correction rather than to give one? And does not the fear of what others think make us hesitate to administer a correction? Regardless of our fear, God sometimes calls us to love by speaking to someone about their behavior. “Speak the truth in love” is the guiding principle of fraternal correction. St. Thomas Aquinas writes of fraternal correction under the aegis of charity, whichshould be administered with prudence, gentleness and discernment.

We see these qualities manifested in Thérèse’s admonition to Sister Martha. Sister Martha was eight years Thérèse’s senior and entered Carmel at the age of twenty-two, four months prior to Thérèse’s entrance. Her parents had died when she was young, and she was shuttled around from orphanage to orphanage throughout her childhood and adolescence. In consequence of a lack of parental bonding that resulted from her childhood deprivation, Sister Martha was emotionally unstable and tended to form intense and erratic relationships. She developed a love/hate relationship with Thérèse; one moment she would serve Thérèse leftovers from the kitchen that no one else would eat, and the next moment she would invite Thérèse to warm herself by the kitchen stove. She both loved and envied Thérèse; she requested to remain in the novitiate in order to benefit from Thérèse’s tutelage but was often sarcastic to Thérèse when she offered advice. Sister Martha’s maternal deprivation also sheds light on why she formed an excessive attachment to Mother Gonzague “as a dog is attached to its master” (S 378). In this chapter (S 376–78) Thérèse writes of the time that she confronted Sister Martha about her relationship with Mother Gonzague. Thérèse, knowing that Sister Martha was a moody person and one “whom [God] grants His light only by degrees... waited patiently,” (S 377) for the right moment to speak to her. How difficult it is to wait in such a situation. Personally, when I know that God is asking me to confront someone about his behavior, I am tempted to rush in and do my duty because I cannot stand the anxiety of waiting. But when we “advance the hour”(S 377), the person whom we are confronting may be less amenable and persuadable to what is being said.

When Thérèse realized that the designated hour had come, she knew that she “must no longer fear to speak out” (S 377). AndThérèse had good reason to fear, for Pauline told her that if Mother Gonzague ever got wind of what she had said to Sister Martha, she risked being sent away to another monastery. “Yes I know,” said Thérèse. “But since I’m now sure that it’s my duty to speak, I cannot let the possible consequences deter me.”[^6]

And when Thérèse finally did speak to Sister Martha, she did so in a gentle and non-judgmental manner. “I begged God to place sweet and convincing words in my mouth... and placing her head upon my heart, told her with tears in my voice _everything I was thinking_ _about her_, but I did this with such tender expressions and showed her such a great affection that very soon her tears were mingled with mine” (S 377). In this incident with Sister Martha we see Thérèse’s _courage_ in speaking the truth, her _patience_ in waiting for the opportune moment and her _gentleness_ in expressing her concern. There is much to reflect upon here. For Thérèse, fraternal correction was always an act of love. May it be the same with us, for there is always the danger that we can use truth as a blunt instrument, under the guise of “tough love.” Honesty and telling the truth are not synonymous; truth is a statement of fact, but honesty is a virtue guided by love. We need to examine our motives before we speak. We need to be sure that we are not using truth in the service of evil. As William Blake writes, “A truth that’s told with bad intent / Beats all the lies you can invent.”[^7]

## Question 3

St. Gregory the Great writes that “The word of reproach is the key that unlocks a door because reproach reveals a fault of which the evildoer is often unaware.”[^8] Do you hold such a key in your hand, and is God asking you to use it for the sake of your neighbor? Conversely, is there someone whom you are strongly tempted to reprove for a wrong reason?[^9]

## Reflection 4

“How great is the power of _Prayer_” (S 384). One night, two months before she died, Thérèse recounted to Pauline the following incident that underlines the efficacy and apostolic nature of prayer. “Sister Marie of the Eucharist wanted to light the candles for a procession; she had no matches; however, seeing the little lamp which was burning in the front of the relics [which was in the infirmary where Thérèse was lying], she approached it. Alas, it was half out; there remained only a feeble glimmer on its blackened wick.

She succeeded in lighting her candle from it, and with this candle, she lighted those of the whole community. It was, therefore, the half extinguished little lamp which had produced all these beautiful flames which, in their turn, could produce an infinity of others and even light the whole universe. Nevertheless, it would always be the little lamp which would be the first cause of all of this light... It is the same with the Communion of Saints. Very often, without our knowing it, the graces and lights that we receive are due to a hidden soul, for God wills that the saints communicate grace to each other through prayer with great love... How often have I thought that I may owe all the graces I’ve received to the prayers of a person who begged them from God for me, and whom I shall only know in heaven. Yes, a very little spark will be capable of giving birth to great lights in the Church, like the Doctors and Martyrs, who will undoubtedly be higher in heaven than the spark... In heaven, we shall not meet with indifferent glances, because all the elect will discover that they owe to each other the graces that merited the crown for them” (LC 99–100).

What an awesome power we possess in prayer as members of the Mystical Body of Christ, even though our prayer be as frail and feeble as a half-extinguished little lamp, even though we are not conscious of the effects that it has upon others. The author of _The_ _Cloud of Unknowing_ writes, “Your fellow men are marvelously enriched by [prayer], even if you may not fully understand how; the souls in purgatory are touched, for their suffering is eased by the effects of this work; and, of course, your own spirit is purified andstrengthened by this contemplative work more than by all others put together.”[^10]

As King Arthur lay dying and breathing his last, he turned to his faithful knight Bedivere and bid him pray for his soul. If thou shouldst never see my face again Pray for my soul. More things are wrought by prayer Than this world dreams of. Wherefore, let thy voice Rise like a fountain for me night and day. For what are men better than sheep or goats That nourish a blind life within the brain, If, knowing God, they lift not hands of prayer Both for themselves and those who call them friend?[^11]

My dear friends, since this is the end of our study guide, I do not wish to close this reflection with a question, as has been our custom, but with a request. As we go our separate ways, let us pray for one another with the belief that prayer is an act of love and that it is love and love alone that helps us on our journey home to God. _Oremus_ _pro invicem_
