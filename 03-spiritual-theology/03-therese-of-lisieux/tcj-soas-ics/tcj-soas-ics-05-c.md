# Background and Context – Chapter V. After the Grace of Christmas

Thérèse was overly sensitive. “I was really unbearable because of my extreme touchiness” (S 151). She had always known this about herself, but as she contemplated entering a religious community, she realized that her touchiness could jeopardize her vocation. “I really don’t know how I could entertain the thought of entering Carmel when I was still in the _swaddling clothes of a child_” (S 152)! Thérèse realized that she had to grow up fast and that God’s grace was necessary for this to happen. “God would have to work a little miracle to make me _grow up_...” (S 152). This miracle happened on Christmas night 1886.

It was the French custom for the baby of the family to have his or her shoes filled with little gifts on Christmas day. This tradition continued in the Martin household until Thérèse was fourteen. When the Martin family returned home from attending midnight mass in 1886, Thérèse’s father noticed her shoes near the fireplace and groaned to Céline, “Thérèse ought to have outgrown all this sort of thing, and I hope this will be the last time.”[^1]

Céline caught Thérèse’s eye and realized that she had overheard what had been said. Aware of her hypersensitivity, Céline followed Thérèse upstairs and found her sitting on her bed with her eyes filled with tears. Céline told her not to go downstairs in this state. But Thérèse knew that she “was no longer the same; Jesus had changed her heart!” Thérèse _acted upon_ this change: “Forcing back my tears, I descended the stairs rapidly; controlling the poundings of my heart, I took my slippers and placed them in front of Papa, and withdrew all the objects joyfully. I had the happy appearance of a queen. Having regained his own cheerfulness, Papa was laughing; Céline believed it was all a dream! Fortunately, it was a sweet reality;Thérèse had discovered once again the strength of soul which she had lost at the age of four and a half, and she was to preserve it forever. On that _night of light_ began the third period of my life. The work I had been unable to do in ten years was done by Jesus in one instant”(S 153).

It is important to realize that Thérèse’s hypersensitivity did not cease to exist. Rather, God had given her the strength to control it. God “made me _strong_ and courageous, arming me with His weapons. Since that night I have never been defeated in any combat. .. The source of my tears was dried up... [and] I received the grace of leaving my childhood, in a word, the grace of my complete conversion” (S 152).

As the fruits of Thérèse’s conversion unfolded, she began to develop in every area of her life. Guy Gaucher writes: The year of 1887 saw her develop in every way: physical maturity and emotional development went hand in hand. _I was_ _at a dangerous age for young girls._ Her desire to love and be loved was great, and her maternal instinct came to the fore when two orphans were looked after at Les Buissonnets... Her intellectual development was also great. “_Freed from scruples and_ _excessive sensitiveness, my mind developed. I had always loved the_ _great and the beautiful, but in this period of my life I was filled with_ _an ardent desire to learn_...” In her attic Thérèse accumulated science and history books. Everything interested her... All her femininity was awakened.[^2]

There was also a spiritual awakening in Thérèse at this time; her zeal for souls was deepened as she “felt _charity_ enter into [her] soul” (S 153). The first manifestation of this new born zeal was her praying for the conversion of the murderer Pranzini; Thérèse referred to him as her “first child,” whom she continued to pray for all of her life.Thérèse had a great desire to enter Carmel on the first anniversary of her Christmas conversion (December 25, 1887), but she did not know whether her father would grant her permission to do so. In addition, Thérèse was hesitant to ask because she knew that it would entail a great sacrifice on his part.

Louis had suffered many losses in his life. His wife had died; his two oldest daughters had entered Carmel; his daughter Léonie was due to enter the convent in July 1887, and on May 1, 1887, he suffered a stroke that paralyzed his entire left side. And now, Thérèse his youngest, whom he doted on, was about to leave him.

Even though Thérèse asked and received her father’s permission to enter Carmel on Pentecost Sunday, May 29, 1887, formidable obstacles stood in her way; both her uncle Isidore and Father Delatroëtte, the ecclesiastical superior of Lisieux Carmel, were adamantly opposed to her entrance at such a tender age. Delatroëtte’s opposition was both intractable and vitriolic. When Thérèse and her father went to speak to him, “He received us coldly ... Nothing could change the superior’s attitude”(S 168), writes Thérèse. Even Father Youf, the chaplain of Lisieux Carmel, was taken back by Delatroëtte’s intransigence.[^3]

After their unsuccessful interview with Delatroëtte, Thérèse and her father went to Bayeux to talk to the bishop. The bishop said neither yes nor no; he said that he would first have to consult with Delatroëtte before making his decision. The chapter ends with Thérèse in Limbo.
