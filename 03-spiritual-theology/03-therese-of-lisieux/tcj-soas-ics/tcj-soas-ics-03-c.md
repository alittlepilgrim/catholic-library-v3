# Background and Context – Chapter III. The Distressing Years

Leaving the family nest for the first time is a precarious adventure.

Cameron and Rychlak write, “The major social change [of school age children] is that, while they are still members of a family they now go forth alone into the world outside their family... The moment a child steps out of the home unaccompanied, he or she loses the status of a protected and privileged son or daughter and becomes merely another child in the neighborhood. The patterns of friendliness, aggression and defense now encountered are different and less predicable by the child than are those to which he or she has been accustomed at home. It is quite normal for a child to be a bit timid and tentative [when placed in this situation].”[^1] If this is the case with children in general, how much more anxiety ridden would Thérèse have been to leave home and venture forth into the world, considering that she was already “timid and retiring [and] could not bear the company of strangers” (S 49)?

When Thérèse was eight years old, she began attending the Benedictine boarding school for girls in Lisieux. School was a painful experience for Thérèse because she had to leave the family circle and enter the rough and tumble world of children. Going to school was the first time that Thérèse had any real interaction with children her own age, except for her cousins Jeanne and Marie Guérin, who lived near Les Buissonnets. Thérèse “didn’t know how to play like other children and as a result wasn’t a very pleasant companion” (S 83) and “_didn’t know_ how to play with dolls” (S 51). These may sound like very insignificant matters to us but in the world of an eight year old girl they are tantamount to saying “I didn’t know how to relate to my peers.” In consequence, Thérèse was shunned. Céline testified:

“The other girls finding [Thérèse] ill-adapted to their games, disregarded her. She did try, but she never succeeded in pleasing them. So she suffered a lot from the rude treatment that was meted out to her. From then on, she liked to keep away and not be seen, sincerely believing herself to be inferior to the others.”[^2]

Because Thérèse had never experienced this pattern of aggression and did not have the tools to defend herself, she withdrew: “I didn’t know how to defend myself and was content to cry without saying a word and without complaining _even to you_ about what I was suffering” (S 82). So reticent was Thérèse to share her sufferings, that her siblings were ignorant of them until many years later. As a result of these experiences, Thérèse began to exhibit signs of depression— sad affect, social withdrawal, and low self esteem. “It was as if a veil had been drawn over all of the fine qualities which the Lord had bestowed on her,” testified Céline.[^3]

But even Thérèse’s unveiled gifts worked against her. They exposed her to the hateful jealousy of one of her classmates. “One of [the students] was about thirteen or fourteen and she wasn’t too intelligent, but she was really adept at influencing the students and even the teachers. When she noticed I was so young, almost always first in the class, and loved by all the sisters, she experienced a jealousy pardonable in a student. She made me pay in a thousand ways for my little successes” (S 81–82). Is it any wonder that Thérèse describes her five years at school as “the saddest in [her] life,” (S 81) years when she “walked sick and sad in the big yard” (S 132)?

But what kept Thérèse from becoming seriously sick at school was her sister Céline, who also attended the boarding school. “If I hadn’t had Céline with me, I couldn’t have remained there and would have become sick in a month” (S 81). These are not empty words, for after Céline graduated from school (Céline was three years older than Thérèse), Louis removed Thérèse from school because he feared that without Céline’s support, Thérèse would relapse into the sickness that she had experienced when she was ten.There was a deep intimacy that existed between Céline and Thérèse. Céline was “the companion of my childhood” (S 23) and “the intimate confidante of my thoughts” (S 158). “Thérèse and I were inseparable,” testifies Céline. “We looked on our elder sisters (Marie and Pauline) more as mothers, but regarded one another as sisters.”[^4] In the springtime of life, these two siblings shared the innocence and simplicity of childhood. “The gifts Marie and Pauline gave their little girls also gave them great joy, though the gifts had no great value. Ah!, it was because we were not _blasé_ at this age; our soul in all its freshness was expanding like a flower content to receive the morning dew” (S 86).

But even the strength of Thérèse’s bond with Céline could not protect Thérèse from the devastating blow that she had received when Pauline, Thérèse’s second mother, left home and entered Carmel. “Pauline was going to leave me... I was about to lose my second mother! Ah! how can I express the anguish of my heart! In one instant, I understood what life was; until then, I had never seen it so sad; but it appeared to me in all its reality, and I saw it was nothing but a continual suffering and separation” (S 88).

Pauline’s departure compounded Thérèse’s sadness and proved to be a burden too heavy to bear. Thérèse began having violent headaches almost immediately after Pauline entered Carmel (Pauline entered Carmel on October 2, 1882, the same day that Thérèse returned to school after the summer vacation.). Over time, Thérèse developed other symptoms—loss of appetite, insomnia, pains in her stomach and side. Thérèse’s condition came to a crisis in the spring of 1883 when Louis Martin took Marie and Léonie to Paris for Holy Week. During this time of separation, Thérèse stayed with her aunt and uncle. “One evening Uncle took me for a walk and spoke about Mama and about past memories with a kindness that touched me profoundly and made me cry.” Later on that evening “when I was undressing, I was seized with a strange trembling...which lasted almost all night” (S 90). For the next six weeks Thérèse was bedridden with “a nervous trembling, followed by seizures of fright and hallucinations which recurred several times a day.[^5]

On Pentecost Sunday she was miraculously cured by the Blessed Virgin but remained psychologically fragile. Thérèse suffered minor relapses after her cure. ^tcj-soas-ics-03-c-1
