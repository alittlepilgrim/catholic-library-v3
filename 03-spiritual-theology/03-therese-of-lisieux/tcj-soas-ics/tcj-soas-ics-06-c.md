# Background and Context – Chapter VI. The Trip to Rome

Thérèse’s trip to Rome was the first and only time that she ever stepped foot outside of her native Normandy. The stated purpose of the pilgrimage was to celebrate Pope Leo XIII’s golden jubilee of ordination to the priesthood, but its unspoken purpose was to make a political statement. The pilgrims were composed of ultramontane Catholics who supported the monarchy and opposed the French Republic and the anti-clerical forces of the day. Italy’s government was deeply anti-clerical, and the pilgrims were harangued at Rome’s train station by protesters who shouted, “Down with Leo XIII! Down with the monarchy!” Going to Rome to celebrate the pope’s jubilee was like carrying a banner in support of the Church and the papacy

But the pilgrimage was also a social event.

The pilgrimage was an excursion of the rich, a sightseeing tour sprinkled with religious overtones; all the shrines, churches, and holy sites of any note were seen on route to Rome. The group stayed in “princely hotels” (S 208) and dined in the finest restaurants. “The nobility... almost exclusively made up the pilgrimage,” (S 189) and seventy five of the total one hundred and ninety seven pilgrims were priests.

At three o’clock in the morning, the Martins left their home in Lisieux to board a train bound for Paris. The stillness of the night made Thérèse reflective. “I crossed the city of Lisieux which was still wrapped in sleep; many impressions passed through my soul at that moment. I had a feeling I was approaching the unknown, that great things awaited me out there” (S 191). The great things that awaited Thérèse were not to be found in shrines or museums but in an inner healing and new found realizations.For example, the high point of Thérèse’s two-day stay in Paris was neither her visit to the Louvre, the Bastille, the Royal Palace, the Arc de Triomphe, or other tourist sites, but a visit to an obscure church—

Our Lady of Victories. It was in this church, four years earlier, when Thérèse was bedridden in Lisieux with her mysterious illness, that a novena of Masses had been said to obtain a cure for her. While the novena was being said, Thérèse was cured. In light of this fact, it is understandable that Thérèse would want to visit Our Lady of Victories in order to render due thanks for her cure. But there was another reason why Thérèse wanted to visit this shrine.

When Thérèse was cured, Marie asked her about her cure. Thérèse told Marie that she had seen the Blessed Virgin smile at her. After Thérèse shared this with Marie, Thérèse felt that she had betrayed a “secret” (S 98). This “betrayal” filled her with a deep sense of guilt for the next four years. “For the next four years... I was unable to look upon myself without a feeling of _profound horror_. I was not to find my happiness again until I was kneeling at the feet of Our Lady of Victories” (S 98–97). When Thérèse knelt before Our Lady of Victories, her feeling of horror vanished as she experienced the intimacy of Mary’s maternal presence. This intimacy was so profound that the deep emotional healing that ensued from it was as miraculous as her cure.” I understood she was watching over me, that I was _her_ child. I could no longer give her any other name but ‘_Mama_’” (S 192).

Thérèse came to other realizations on the pilgrimage as she saw the world of the rich up close for the first time. She was not impressed by their snobbish superficiality. “Ah!, far from dazzling us, all these titles and these “_de_” appeared to us as nothing but smoke. From a distance, this had sometimes thrown a little powder in my eyes, but close up I saw that “all that glistens is not gold” (S 190). In contrast to the haughty ostentatiousness of the rich, Thérèse saw true nobility of spirit manifested in the “habitual kindness” ofher father, who patiently related to a malcontent, who complained constantly. Likewise, Thérèse’s eyes were opened to the worldliness of priests who hobnobbed with the wealthy. From a distance they appeared to her to be “as pure as crystal,” but up close, she saw them as “weak and fragile men” (S 190).

Thérèse’s main purpose for going to Rome was to obtain permission from Pope Leo XIII to enter Carmel at the age of fifteen, but all she received from him was an exhortation to obedience and faith: “Well, my child... do what the superiors tell you!... Go... go... _Y ou will enter if God wills it_” (S 205). Bitterness filled her soul, but Thérèse was also at peace with herself because she knew that she had done everything God had asked her to do.

When the Martins arrived back in Lisieux, they went almost immediately to Carmel only to discover that Father Delatroëtte was as obstinate as ever in his opposition to Thérèse’s request to enter Carmel on Christmas day. But on New Year’s day, Thérèse received news from Mother Gonzague that the bishop had given her permission to allow Thérèse to enter immediately. All seemed well. But now the opposition came from _within_ Carmel. Thérèse’s sister Pauline had second thoughts. She feared that the Lenten fast would be too stressful for Thérèse and persuaded Mother Gonzague to postpone Thérèse’s entrance for three months.[^1]

This infuriated Louis Martin. He came to Carmel and “pointed his finger, saying, ‘She (Pauline) is always the same; she says something, then she takes it back.’ Oh! He was angry...”[^2] Thérèse was also upset about the postponement, but she did not put her life on hold, rather she continued to lead “a life as well regulated as had been [her] custom,” and in the process, came to learn “the value of time” (S 216).

---

[^1]: Another factor that may have pressured Mother Gonzague to postpone Thérèse’s entrance date was Father Delatroëtte’s opposition. Postponing Thérèse’s entrance date may have been an attempt to placate his anger.

[^2]: This is the testimony of Marie Martin (Sister Marie of the Sacred Heart), quoted from Patricia O’Connor, _Thérèse of Lisieux_ (Huntington: Our Sunday Visitor, Inc., 1983), 48.
