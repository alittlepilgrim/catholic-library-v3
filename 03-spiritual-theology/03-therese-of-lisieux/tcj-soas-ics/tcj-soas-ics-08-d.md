# Reflections and Questions – Chapter VIII. Profession and Offering to Merciful Love

## Reflection 1

Retreats made Thérèse vulnerable to attacks of scruples because they revived emotionally laden memories of the retreat that had triggered her first attack of scruples. A similar dynamic is true in our own lives. Whenever we experience something in the present that resembles a traumatic event from our past, old memories and their attending feelings are reactivated. And like Thérèse, whenever we are in the vice grip of deeply-seated fears and insecurities, we need someone who can help us to calm our anxieties, to quell our insecurities, and to put things in perspective for us. And like Thérèse, we need to be told the same things, over and over and over again.

## Question 1

Who are the loving, patient, understanding people in your life whom you go to in your hour of need? Conversely, who are the people whom God asks you to minister to in like manner?

## Reflection 2

Thérèse did not expect that Father Alexis would be able to help her with her trials, for as she wrote, “it seemed to me that the preacher would not be able to understand me since he was supposed to do good to great sinners but not to religious souls” (S 269). Thérèse was wrong. The person whom Thérèse thought would not be able to help her proved to be one of the greatest graces of her life.

## Question 2

How often in your life has God used the most unlikely person to minister to you in your hour of need? How often has a chance encounter or a parenthetical remark proved to be the means by which God’s providence has changed your life?

## Reflection 3

St. Teresa of Ávila exhorted her nuns to speak to God in prayer “as with a father, or a brother, or a lord, or as with a spouse; sometimes in one way, at other times in another; He will teach you what you must do in order to please Him.”[^8] We see Teresa’s advice manifested in the opening paragraphs of chapter eight of _Story of a_ _Soul_, where Thérèse writes of two ways by which she related to God. The first is that of Jesus sleeping in the bow of a boat. The second is that of a loving parent who understands his or her child’s frailties. It is instructive to notice _when_ Thérèse used each image.

a) _Jesus asleep._ When Thérèse was in a state of aridity and felt abandoned by God, she imaged Jesus being asleep in her presence. This image helped Thérèse to bear her desolation because it took her mind off herself and focused it on God. “Since the most absolute aridity and almost total abandonment were my lot, Jesus was sleeping as usual in my little boat; ah! I see very well how rarely souls allow Him to sleep peacefully within them. Jesus is so fatigued with always having to take the initiative and to attend to others that he hastens to take advantage of the repose _I offer to Him_” (S 259) (italic added).

Let us apply this image to ourselves. Place yourself in a boat with the person whom you love the most, as Thérèse did. You are in the middle of the ocean; it is night; you are lonely and your beloved is fast asleep. You want to wake him in order to relieve your loneliness but decide to let him sleep because he is exhausted. As you gaze at his loving face, your loneliness is transfigured; you are still lonely but something has changed. Mysteriously, your self-pity has vanished; it has been purged away by love. As St. Teresa writes: “Oh, how everything that is suffered with love is healed again!”[^9]

b) _God as a Loving Parent._ Thérèse used the image of God as a loving parent to counteract her tendency toward discouragement. “I should be desolate for having slept (for seven years) during my hours of prayer and my _thanksgivings_ after Holy Communion; well, I am not desolate. I remember that _little children_ are as pleasing to their parents when they are asleep as well as when they are wide awake” (S 259–60).

In the monastery, we Carmelite friars often use a phrase to describe our hours of mental prayer—“mental sleep.” The phrase refers to the common experience of falling asleep during meditation. Even though I find it very comforting to know that I have something in common with Thérèse, what I really want to share with her is not that _I fall asleep_ during prayer but what she said to God when she _woke up_.

When we wake up from our “mental sleep” a barrage of castigating words directed toward ourselves and a litany of remorse offered up to God are not helpful. In fact, they are downright harmful. They focus our minds on ourselves and paint in our imaginations an image of an over-sensitive God, who stands upon his dignity and takes offense where no offense is given. Would you be angry at a small child who fell asleep on your lap in church? Rather, wouldn’t you be more inclined to look down lovingly on such a tender sight? This is how Thérèse imagined that God looked down upon her during her “mental sleep.” “I remember that _little_ _children_ are as pleasing to their parents when they are asleep as well as when they are wide awake.” In the light of this truth, would it not be more appropriate to say to God as you’re waking up from your “mental sleep,” “O, thank you God for the nap, I really needed it.” Both images—Jesus asleep in the bow of the boat and God as a parent who gazes down lovingly upon his child—have this in common; they take our minds off ourselves and help us to focus them on God.

## Question 3

Do you use different images of God during prayer? Why do you use them? How do they function in prayer? Regarding her habit of falling asleep during prayer, Thérèse transformed false guilt into gratitude. Are there areas in your life (besides falling asleep during prayer) where you can do the same?
