# Background and Context – Chapter II. Les Buissonnets

In 1865, Zélie Martin noticed a swelling in her breast. For eleven long years, her condition was not taken seriously by doctors until it was too late. She was diagnosed with incurable breast cancer. Toward the end, when Zélie’s pain was so great that she could no longer repress her cries, Thérèse and Céline (ages four and seven, respectively) were removed from the Martin household during the day. They spent the day at the home of Madame Leriche, Monsieur Martin’s niece by marriage. Zélie’s death was a brutal shock for Thérèse. It so traumatized her that Thérèse withdrew into herself and clung to her family.

On her death bed, Zélie gave her last look neither to her husband nor to any of her children but to her sister-in-law Céline Guérin. “I believed that I understood that look” [Céline Guérin wrote to Thérèse] “which nothing will be able to make me forget. It is engraved within my heart. Since that day, I have tried to replace her whom God had taken away from you, but, alas! nothing can replace a mother” (L 745)! Céline Guérin understood that Zélie was asking her to take care of her children. The Guérins took this request very seriously. Isidore Guérin was named the deputy guardian of the Martin children, and the Guérins proposed to the Martins that they move to Lisieux where they lived. The Martins agreed.

The move to Lisieux proved to be a good one for the Martin family. Both _Story of a Soul_ and the family correspondence paint an idyllic picture of the Martin family during its early years in Lisieux.

Thérèse’s account of herself during this time gives the impression that these were some of the happiest years of her life.

The whole atmosphere that surrounded the Martin family in Lisieux was much more joyous and serene than the one they had lived in at Alençon, What made the difference? There is no one factor that accounts for this change, for a psychic atmosphere like a physical one is composed of many particles. Let us explore some of the elements that constituted the new atmosphere that the Martin family enjoyed in Lisieux.

Before the Martin family arrived in Lisieux, Isidore procured a spacious well-lighted house for them to rent. It was cloistered by a ring of trees and walls, and sequestered on the outskirts of town.

“From the house you are not seen from any direction,” wrote Isidore to Louis, “and you see no road but simply a delightful panorama of the town” (L 128). What added to this contemplative atmosphere was the beauty of the grounds. “It is a charming house, cheerful and gay,” wrote Marie to her father, “with a large garden in which Céline and Thérèse will be able to play” (L 130). Their new home, which was promptly christened Les Buissonnets (“little bushes”) by the Martin children because of the little bushes that surrounded the house, was adjacent to a beautiful spacious park, the _Jardin de l’Étoile_ which was a source of sheer delight for Thérèse. “This baby loves flowers so much... she’s very soon going to pick all there are of lilies of the valley in the “_jardin de l’Étoile_” (L 135). Thérèse, who was deeply nourished by the beauty of nature, came home to herself at Les Buissonnets.

Not only was Les Buissonnets sequestered from public view, so was the Martin family. Except for church functions, the Martins rarely participated in the social life of Lisieux. They spend most evenings at home gathered around the family hearth listening to Louis either singing, reading from Dom Guéranger’s _The Liturgical__Year_, or reciting “from memory long passages of poetry principally from Lamartine and Victor Hugo.”[^1]

The peaceful, bucolic atmosphere that permeated Les Buissonnets was in stark contrast to the noisy urban atmosphere that had surrounded the Martin household in Alençon. Their house opened on to a busy city street and was a thoroughfare for customers and employees associated with Zélie’s lace business. Because their living space was also a place of employment, privacy and silence were at a premium.

Thérèse lived a very sheltered life and an overprotected existence at Les Buissonnets, yet, that is what she needed to survive those early years that immediately followed her mother’s death. “Ah! If God had not showered His beneficent rays upon His little flower,” writes Thérèse, commenting on her family’s care for her after the death of her mother, “she could never have accustomed herself to earth, for she was too weak to stand up against the rains and the storms. She needed warmth, a gentle dew, and the springtime breezes” (S 49–50). But as we will see in the next chapter, the medicinal atmosphere of Les Buissonnets came at a price.
