# Background and Context – Chapter VII. The First Years in Carmel

On April 9, 1888 “casting a last look upon Les Buissonnets, that beautiful cradle of [her] childhood which [she] was never to see again,” Thérèse left home and entered Lisieux Carmel. It was a deeply emotional day for Thérèse. The sadness of leaving her family behind, coupled with the joy and excitation of being received into “the holy ark” (S 230) made her heart beat “_so violently_ it seemed impossible to walk when they signaled [Thérèse] to come to the enclosure door” (S 230).

When Thérèse crossed the threshold, Father Delatroëtte said caustically, “Well! Reverend Mothers, you can sing a Te Deum! As the bishop’s delegate I give you this fifteen-year-old child whose entrance you have wished for. I hope she does not disappoint your hopes, but I remind you that if she does you alone will bear responsibility.”[^1] Twenty years later, Thérèse’s relatives remembered with indignation, this biting remark. We do not know if Thérèse overheard what was said, for she only records the joyful memories of that day. She tells us that her soul “experienced a _PEACE_ so sweet, so deep... [that for] seven years and a half that inner peace [had] not abandoned [her] in the midst of the greatest trials” (S 230). The first trial that Thérèse encountered in Carmel was the makeup of the community. Between Thérèse and the majority of the nuns, “there were such differences in temperament, social background and everyday behavior!”[^2] The majority of the nuns were ill-educated peasant women who lacked the social refinements and mental endowments common to the culture in which Thérèse was reared.

Thérèse found their “lack of judgment, good manners and touchiness” (S 389) difficult to endure. In short, we might say, that Thérèse, who was raised in an upper middle class bourgeois culture, now found herself living in a “blue collar” neighborhood.

The contrast between Thérèse’s upbringing (both socially and personally) and that of the majority of the nuns was clearly evident in the area of work. Having been raised in the leisure class, “the grand lady,” as Thérèse was called by one of the nuns, lacked domestic skills and was unaccustomed to doing simple household tasks. Thérèse was very slow and awkward when it came to sewing and sweeping a corridor, the two main tasks to which she was assigned as a postulant. Sister Saint Vincent de Paul, who nicknamed Thérèse, “the big nanny goat,” because of her slowness when it came to manual labor, told Thérèse’s novice mistress that Thérèse would never be of use to the community. Once, when Thérèse entered the laundry room, Sister Saint Vincent de Paul said, “Here she comes! She certainly is in no hurry! When is she going to get to work?” Thérèse did not fire back with a cutting quip (of which she was very capable, for she had a quick wit), but simply smiled. In spite of her patience, Thérèse felt the smart of the barbs, jabs, and cutting remarks that came her way.

“We don’t have any enemies in Carmel, but there are feelings” (S 349). But negative feelings were not the only emotions that proved to be a trial for Thérèse when she entered Carmel.

On entering Carmel, Thérèse was reunited with the two women who had mothered her as a child (Pauline and Marie). Even though Thérèse resisted their attempts to mother her, she found it difficult to do so because her desire to be mothered was very strong. “I didn’t come to Carmel to live with my sisters but to answer Jesus’s call. Ah! I really felt in advance that this living with one’s own sisters had to be the cause of continual suffering when one wishes to grant nothing to one’s natural inclinations” (S 338). Her desire to receive maternal succoring was also transferred to the mother prioress. “I remember when I was still a postulant that I had such violent temptations to satisfy myself and to find a few crumbs of pleasure that I was obliged to walk rapidly by your [Mother Gonzague’s] door and to cling firmly to the banister of the staircase in order not to turn back. There came into my mind a crowd of permissions to seek; in a word, dear Mother, I found a thousand reasons for pleasing my nature” (S 378).

In short, Thérèse’s entrance into Carmel required that she “leave

home” in two ways. First, she had to learn how to live with people who had come from a different background than she had; second, she had to resist the temptation to reestablish old familiar roles with mother figures, namely her older siblings and Mother Gonzague. “What would have become of me if I had become the ‘pet’ of the community, as some of the sisters believed” (S 233)? But as painful as Thérèse’s struggle was not to become the baby of the community, it was nothing in comparison to what she had to endure regarding her father’s illness.

On June 23, two months after Thérèse entered Carmel, her beloved

father suddenly disappeared from Les Buissonnets and was found four days later in Le Havre with the fixed idea that he wanted to live as a hermit. Louis Martin’s mental confusion was the result of physical and emotional factors; he was suffering from arteriosclerosis and a kidney infection, and he was mourning the loss of his children. Thérèse had entered Carmel on April 9, Céline had informed her father on June 15 of her desire to do the same, and Léonie had made plans to reenter the Visitation convent at Caen. “Oh, my children are abandoning me,” sobbed Louis.[^3]

As time went on, Louis’ mental condition became so unstable that it became questionable whether or not he would be able to attend Thérèse’s clothing ceremony (January 10, 1889). He did, and Thérèse called this day “his day of _triumph_,” (S 239) but the triumph was short lived. Within a fortnight, Louis became delusional. He heard the sounds of battles and screams of slaughter and began to carry a revolver in order to defend his family. When he was finally disarmed by his brother-in-law Isidore Guérin, Louis was institutionalized in Bon Sauveur mental asylum in Caen on February 12, 1889. This trial was almost more than Thérèse could bear. “I recall that in the month of June 1888, at the moment of our first trials [the time of Louis’ flight to Le Havre], I said: ‘I am suffering very much, but I feel I can still bear greater trials.’ I was not thinking then of the ones reserved for me. I didn’t know that on February 12, a month after my reception of the habit, our dear father would drink the _most bitter_ and _most humiliating_ of all chalices” (S 240–41). Louis stayed in Bon Sauveur for the next three years.

Thérèse was due to make her final vows in January 1890, but the date was postponed until September because Mother Gonzague felt that if Thérèse requested that she make her vows in January, Father Delatroëtte would oppose the request. The nine months wait was difficult but purifying for Thérèse, for in the process of waiting, she realized how much self-will was contained in making her vows according to her own timetable. In September, Thérèse made her final commitment. She hoped that her father would be able to attend; that hope was never realized.

On May 10, 1892, Isidore Guérin traveled to Caen and brought Louis back to Lisieux. May 12 was the last time that Thérèse ever saw her father. It was a heartrending encounter for Thérèse. Louis, once so handsome and dignified, was now gaunt and his faculties were diminished. He was almost mute; when he spoke, he did so only in phrases, “My little queen,” etc. As he left in tears, Louis pointed upwards and said, “_Au ciel_” (“In heaven!”). Heaven, was the first word that Thérèse had ever read (S 51), and it was the last word that she ever heard her father speak.
