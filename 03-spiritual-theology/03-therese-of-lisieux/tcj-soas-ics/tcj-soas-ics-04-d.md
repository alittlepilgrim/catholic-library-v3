# Reflections and Questions – Chapter IV. First Communion, Boarding School

## Reflection 1

Thérèse tells us that she loved reading “and would have spent [her] life at it.” And even though what she read “nourished both [her] heart and [her] mind,” she put limits on how much she read; “I was not to go beyond a certain time in my reading, which was the cause of great sacrifices for me as I had to interrupt my reading very often at the most enticing passage.” (S 114). There are two related issues here. With _what_ do we feed our minds, and _how much_ time do we expend in doing so?

## Question 1

**_Questions._** a) We live in a noisy world that clamors for our attention. We are assaulted with a barrage of billboards, flashing neon signs, those annoying “pop up” advertisements on the internet, pesky telemarketers, hackers with handbills and leaflets stuffed under the windshield wipers of our cars. Each year Madison Avenue spends billions of dollars on advertisement based upon the assumption that what they can present to our senses will inculcate desire and change our behavior, namely, to buy their product. But advertisement does more than promote its product; it preaches a philosophy of life. It whispers, “In order to be happy, in order to livebthe good life, you must have me, use me, taste me, try me, drink me, etc.”

And what is true of advertisement is true of almost everything that our modern world presents to our senses. The music we listen to, the books we read, the television programs we watch, all communicate to us a philosophy of life. Plato taught that music shapes our character because it shapes the impulses of the soul. Or as Josef Pieper puts it, “Music may be nothing but a secret philosophizing of the soul.”[^5] Music philosophizes the soul not by means of concepts but by the emotional impress that it leaves upon us, either for good or for ill. Music will either lighten or darken our mood, soften or harshen our human sensitivities. And what is true of music is also true of what we read and watch on television. For example, what effect do you believe it would have upon you if you watched the Jerry Springer show all day long, every day of the week? How do you think it would influence your vision of life? How do you think it would shape the way you view human nature? Would it nourish or deplete your humanity? Thérèse tells us that what she read _nourished_ her heart and her mind. Is this true for you?

b) If Washington Irving had written _Rip Van Winkle_ in our day, he might have opened his story with a man with a long white beard signing off on the internet, with a startled look on his face, as he reads, “You have just spent 20 years online.” Many of us have had similar experiences. For example, you log on the internet _just_ to read your email when some question of curiosity pops into your mind. “I wonder what ever happened to William “The Refrigerator” Perry who played defensive tackle for the Chicago Bears?” As you spend a few minutes checking out various web sites in order to satisfy this curiosity, another question comes to your mind. “Was it Emily or Charlotte Brontë who wrote _Jane Eyre_? I can never remember which Brontë sister wrote what!”[^6] And so on, and so on, and so on. And before you know it, half the morning is shot. “You just spent 97 minutes online.” Thérèse tells us that it was difficult for her to put a time limit on her reading because it often entailed stopping “at the most enticing passage” (S 114). How true to life is Thérèse’s experience! How difficult it is to stop an activity in which we are engrossed. But perhaps what is more pertinent to us in our overstimulated, bored culture is setting boundaries to anesthetizing activities. For example, when I find myself “surfing the net,” it is usually because I am restless or bored; I don’t know what to do with myself, so I indulge in a little diversion. Such mindless activity (“surfing the net” may better be termed as “drifting with the tide” or “aimless wandering,” for surfing involves skill and concentration) consumes precious time which could be used for activities that nourish the soul. Our time is limited, and how we spend it is a basic choice in life. What “mindless” activities do I need to limit in order to have time to engage in behaviors that will nourish my soul (doing spiritual reading, communing with nature, sitting in silence, listening to classical music, etc.)?

## Reflection 2

“Fidelity in little things” became a hallmark of Thérèse’s spirituality because her realism told her that true love consists of concrete acts of charity. Fidelity in little things is attending to life as we find it and loving the people that God has placed upon our path. As an adolescent, Thérèse had fantasies and great desires to serve God by means of heroic deeds like Joan of Arc but she came to understand that her “own _glory_ would not be evident to the eyes of mortals, that it would consist in becoming a great _saint_” (S 115) through a life of love lived out in the shadows. Thérèse was aware of the temptation to flee from the hard work that love demands by escaping into dreams of glory, of which St. Teresa of Ávila writes: “The devil gives us great desires so that we will avoid setting ourselves to the task at hand, serving our Lord in possible things, and instead be content with having desired the impossible.”[^7] It is a hard fact to accept that our “own glory” will be enacted under the guise of the ordinary and consist in being faithful in fulfilling the lackluster obligations of our station in life. Yet magnanimity lies within the narrow circumference of the mundane, for fidelity to the daily round requires the fortitude of the long haul. “Faithfulness is consecration in overalls,” writes Evelyn Underhill, “It is the steady acceptance and performance of the common duty and immediate task without any reference to personal preferences... Faithfulness means continuing quietly with the job we have been given, in the situation where we have been placed; not yielding to the restless desire for change. It means tending the lamp quietly for God without wondering how much longer it has got to go on.”[^8] Faithfulness requires endurance, and endurance is the essence of fortitude says St. Thomas; it is the loving service that holds down the fort, it is the undramatic heroism of the unsung, uncelebrated, unacknowledged, and unrecognized. Such was the existence of Thérèse, whose glory was not evident to the eyes of the world but consisted in practicing the ordinary virtues. Such are our own lives.

**_Questions._** How do you see what you do both at home and at work? Do you see your duties (e.g., house work, etc) simply as tasks to be done or as the context in which God calls you to the holiness of love? For example, let us say that you are a checkout clerk at a supermarket. Do you define your work as merely ringing up items on the cash register and bagging groceries, or do you see that the real work that God invites you to is being patient and kind to the people you serve? What helps you sustain a faith vision of the eternal significance of what you do in the midst of the mundane? What hinders?3. Thérèse deeply believed in the truth contained in the old saying “There but for the grace of God go I.” “I know that without Him, I could have fallen as low as St. Mary Magdalene... I also know that Jesus _has forgiven me more than St. Mary Magdalene_ since He forgave me _in advance_ by preventing me from falling” (S 128). In the opening scene of Dante’s _Inferno_, Dante wakes up to the realization that he is lost in a forest of sin and ignorance. He tells us that he has awakened just in the nick of time. In this regard, Dante employs the image of a man who has barely escaped with his life from a shipwreck. “And as one who, with laboring breath/has escaped from the deep to the shore/turns and looks back at the perilous waters/ my mind, still in flight! turned back to look once more upon the pass/no mortal being ever left alive.”[^9] Dante looked back over his life and reflected upon his narrow escape from moral disaster. Similarly, Thérèse was conscious of what her life would have been if God’s grace had not prevented her from falling.

## Question 2

Reflect upon the crossroads of your life. Think of how different your life would be, if God had not prevented you from making a disastrous choice. T.S. Eliot writes of “a moment’s surrender which an age of prudence can never retract.”[^10] Think of how close you came to surrendering your will to a deed that could never be undone. And then thank God for his merciful love and know anew: “There but for the grace of God go I.”
