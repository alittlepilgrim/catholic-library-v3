# Background and Context – Chapter X. The Trial of Faith

One night, four months before Thérèse died, Pauline could not sleep; there was something that she needed to tell the prioress and it couldn’t wait. “Towards midnight on June 2, 1897, four months before Sister Thérèse’s death, I went to Mother Prioress’s room and said: ‘Mother, I cannot sleep until I tell you a certain secret. While I was prioress, Sister Thérèse wrote some memoirs of her childhood. She did so out of obedience to please me. The other day I read them through again and they are very charming but... [they] hardly say anything about her life as a nun. Now, if you were to order her to do so, she could write something more serious.[^1] The next day, Mother Gonzague commanded Thérèse to write about her experiences in Carmel; the result was chapters ten and eleven ([[tcj-soas-ics-10-a|Manuscript C]]) of _Story of a Soul_.

It was the worst of times for Thérèse to write. “She set to work, and wrote as it came to her... It was quite disorderly, however, because of her illness and the interruptions caused by the constant coming and going of the infirmarians and the novices, these latter taking as much advantage as they could of her last days with them. So much so that one day she said to me: ‘I don’t know what I’m writing... I am writing about charity, but I have not been able to do it as well as I should have liked to; in fact, I couldn’t have done it worse if I’d tried.’ She stopped writing at the beginning of July 1897. She was so weak that she had to use a pencil for the last page.”[^2] Chapter ten deals with several aspects of Thérèse’s spiritual life but focuses on her trial of faith and charity.

a) _Thérèse’s Trial of Faith._ A month after Thérèse made her last private retreat (the retreat during which she wrote a summary of her “little way” for her sister Marie), the community’s retreat took place (October 8–15, 1896).[^3] During this retreat, Thérèse confided to the retreat master Father Godefroy Madelaine that she had been experiencing doubts in faith for the past six months; he advised Thérèse to always carry a copy of the Creed on her person and place her hand on it whenever these doubts arose in her mind. Thérèse followed this advice and wrote out the Creed in her own blood.

Thérèse’s trial of faith began on Easter Sunday, April 5, 1896, two days after her first hemoptysis. Doubts of all kinds began to plague her. “I do not believe in eternal life. It seems to me that after this mortal life, there is nothing any more.” “Last evening, I was seized with a veritable anguish and my darkness increased. [A] voice said to me. ‘Are you sure of being loved by God? Did he come and tell you?’”[^4] “It seems to me that the darkness, borrowing the voice of sinners, says mockingly to me: ‘You are dreaming about the light, about a fatherland embalmed in the sweetest perfumes; you are dreaming about the _eternal_ possession of the Creator of all these marvels; you believe that one day you will walk out of this fog which surrounds you! Advance, advance; rejoice in death which will give you not what you hope for but a night still more profound, the night of nothingness” (S 335). “I want to do good after my death, but I will not be able to do so! It will be as it was for Mother Geneviève: We expected to see her work in miracles, and complete silence fell over her tomb” (LC 257–58). “Frightful thoughts obsess me!... It’s the reasoning of the worst materialists which is imposed upon my mind” (LC 257). “[Science is] unceasingly making new advances, science will explain everything naturally; we shall have the absolute reason for everything that exists and that still remains a problem” (LC 257). In short, Thérèse’s doubts were expressive of the scientific age in which she lived, an age in which that which could not be verified by experimentation or empirical evidence was called into question, an age in which metaphysics and the claims of religion were scoffed at, an age of skepticism and suspicion of all that laid claim to the supernatural.

Thérèse was knowledgeable of history and science, the two great subjects of her day. “I applied myself to some special studies in _history and science,_ and I did this on my own. The other studies left me indifferent, but these two subjects attracted all my attention; in a few months I acquired more knowledge than during my years of study” (S 156). Thérèse thought about what her contemporaries were saying about life after death and wondered if what they said was true. As a reflective child, Thérèse spent long periods of times thinking about the ultimate questions of existence. “I _thought..._ I think about God, about life, about ETERNITY _..._ I _think_” (S 117).

Thérèse _believed_ in the promise of eternal life but she had her _doubts._ And these doubts were not peculiar to her trial of faith that occurred at the end of her life. “At that time I was having great interior trials of all kinds, even to the point of asking myself whether heaven really existed” (S 269). The time was 1891, five years prior to the onset of her trial of faith.

Thérèse shared the obdurate doubts and anxieties of her contemporaries and in the process grew in understanding and compassion for the agnostics and atheists of her age, whom she referred to as “her brothers” (S 334) and once referred to Nietzsche as her “sick little brother.” She also entered into the darkness of people who freely rejected the light, “who, through the abuse of grace, lost this precious treasure, the source of the only real and pure joys” (S 333).

While we do not know the exact nature of Thérèse’s trial of faith, suffice it to say, that while Thérèse was growing in _the virtue of faith,_ she was devoid of the _consolation of faith._ “I do not have _the joy of_ _faith_, I am trying to carry out its works at least” (S 335).

b) _Charity._ There are three fundamental truths about charity that Thérèse addresses in this chapter. The first is that charity is a not a feeling but a choice. “I told myself that charity must not consist in feelings but in works” (S 346). The second is that it is very difficult to be charitable to people whom we don’t like, people whom we have a “natural antipathy”(S 346) toward, to use Thérèse phrase. Third, it is precisely in loving those to whom we have a natural aversion, that our love becomes divine because such love requires that we love with the charity of Jesus himself. Thérèse writes of two nuns whom she had a natural aversion toward but whom she chose to love with the love of Jesus—Sister Marie of St. Joseph and Sister Teresa of St. Augustine.

_Sister Marie of St. Joseph._ During the darkest days of Thérèse’s life, when her body was being consumed by tuberculosis and her soul was submerged in a dark night of faith, she _volunteered_ to work in the linen closet with Sister Marie of St. Joseph, a woman with a depressive temperament who was prone to such outbursts of anger that none of the nuns would work with her. In due course, Sister Marie lived on the fringe of the community.[^5]

Thérèse worked with Sister Marie from March 1896 to May 1897. In June 1897, Thérèse wrote the following: “This year _..._ God has given me the grace to understand what charity is; I understood it before, it is true, but in an imperfect way... I understood how imperfect was my love for my sisters. I saw that I didn’t love them as God loves them. Ah! I understand now that charity consists in bearing with the faults of others... I understood _..._ that charity must not remain hidden in the bottom of the heart [but is a lamp that is placed upon the lamp stand] _so as to give light to ALL in the house_... All who are _in the house_” without distinction” (S 343–44).

Thérèse once compared Sister Marie to “an old clock that has to be rewound every quarter of an hour,”[^6] and said that if she “had an infirmity such as hers and so defective a spirit... [she] would despair.”[^7] Even for Thérèse, Sister Marie was a difficult person to love.

_Sister Teresa of St. Augustine._ If we reflect upon our experience, we will find that there are certain people to whom we have a natural aversion—an aversion that is so irrational that it defies explanation. “[God’s] hand is so gentle!... But creatures!... Those who surround me are very good, but there is something, I don’t know what, that repels me!... I cannot give you any explanation” (L 499–500).[^8] Sister Teresa of St. Augustine was such a person. She had a rigid personality that reminded Thérèse of a “potted lily.” “There is in the community a sister who has the faculty of displeasing me in everything, in her ways, her words, her character, everything seems _very disagreeable_ to me” (S 346). Thérèse _felt_ a natural antipathy toward Sister Teresa of St. Augustine, but this did not prevent Thérèse from acting in love. “I told myself that charity must not consist in feelings but in works; then I set myself to doing for this sister what I would do for the person I loved the most” (S 346). So well did Thérèse mask her feelings, that it was only years after Thérèse’s death that Sister Teresa of St. Augustine came to the sobering realization that she was the person whom Thérèse wrote about in _Story of a Soul_.

c) _Drawing Profit From Everything._ Thérèse wrote that God granted her the grace “to draw profit from everything” (L 795). We see this in an incident where Thérèse was rashly judged as being unwilling to help another nun bring the community Christmas tree through the garden gate (S 345). When Thérèse was rash judged, she did not become resentful, but rather she became more “indulgent toward the weaknesses of others” (S 345).

d) _Perspective._ One of the graces that interrelated with Thérèse’s ability to draw profit from every situation was her ability to see the “little things” of life in perspective. “Alas! when I think of the time of novitiate I see how imperfect I was. I made so much fuss over such little things that it makes me laugh now” (S 348). Thérèse, even got to the point in her spiritual development where she was able to laugh at being misjudged. “When we’re misunderstood and judged unfavorably, what good does it do to defend or explain ourselves? Let the matter drop and say nothing. It’s so much better to say nothing and allow others to judge us as they please!... O blessed silence that gives so much peace to souls” (LC 36)!

[^3]: Godefroy Madelaine, O. Praem preached the annual retreat of Lisieux Carmel in 1890, 1892, and 1896. He was very instrumental in editing and publishing _Story of a Soul_. A month after Thérèse died, Mother Gonzague asked Madelaine if he would read over the autobiographical writings of Thérèse and give his opinion of them. Madelaine was impressed by what he had read and was convinced that the manuscript should be published. He obtained permission for its publication from the bishop of Bayeux (Flavien Hugonin, the same bishop who had received Thérèse and her father in 1887 and who had presided at the reception of her habit in 1889) but only after he convinced the bishop that the manuscript was not the product of “the female imagination.” “What happened was this,” testified Madelaine, “When the bishop heard of a manuscript by Sister Thérèse, his first reaction was one of distrust of the female imagination. But I was able to assure him in all conscience that I had studied the matter carefully and that I had been forced,... to recognize that this whole manuscript bore evident marks of the Spirit of God, and that I had been unable to find any doctrinal error in it. On the strength of this testimony, the bishop granted his _imprimatur_.” (O’Mahony, 273–74).

[^5]: Shortly before Thérèse died, Sister Marie of St. Joseph placed a violet on the window sill next to Thérèse’s bed as a sign of her love. The day after Thérèse died, Sister Marie smelled the scent of violets in her room. As she remembered the violet that she had placed on Thérèse’s window sill two weeks before, she realized that the scent was a gift from Thérèse.

In 1909, when she was fifty-five years old, after spending twenty-eight years in religious life, Sister Marie of St. Joseph left Lisieux Carmel because her mental condition had deteriorated. She died in 1936 at the age of seventy-eight after wandering aimlessly throughout the whole of France. However, she kept in touch with Lisieux Carmel. In 1929, she wrote the following to Pauline (Mother Agnes). “The work of sanctification which my beloved Thérèse began so lovingly in me before she died still continues. And I can say in all sincerity that ‘my house is at rest.’ And I live now in complete abandonment. As long as I love Jesus, and He and Thérèse are pleased, nothing else matters to me.”

When Sister Marie left Lisieux Carmel, there was something that she did not leave behind—the poem that Thérèse had written for her (_The Eternal Canticle Sung Even in Exile_). In the years that followed her departure, the word _Exile_ proved to have symbolic significance far beyond what Thérèse had ever intended. I like to think that during her wanderings, the poem was like manna in the desert for Sister Marie, that it was one of the means through which Thérèse’s love continued to be Sister Marie’s daily bread. But beyond the sacrament of the poem, Thérèse’s interceding presence continued to be with Sister Marie until her death.

[^6]: O’Mahony, _Those Who Knew Her_, 94.

[^8]: The nun to whom Thérèse is referring in this letter is Sister Saint Vincent de Paul. She was of a practical nature and had no patience for Thérèse who was very clumsy and slow in doing any type of manual labor. Sister Saint Vincent de Paul nicknamed Thérèse “the big clumsy goat” and often gave voice to her feelings: “Look at her; she is in no hurry; when will she ever start the washing; she’s good for nothing.” Once, after Thérèse was fitted for a pair of sandals by Sister Saint Vincent de Paul, Thérèse wrote to Pauline. “This morning, I had trouble with Sister Saint Vincent de Paul; I went away with a heavy heart”(L 503). But in spite of Sister Saint Vincent de Paul’s gruffness, she had a good heart, edified the community by long hours before the Blessed Sacrament, and did not hesitate to give voice to the extraordinary virtue that she saw in Thérèse.
