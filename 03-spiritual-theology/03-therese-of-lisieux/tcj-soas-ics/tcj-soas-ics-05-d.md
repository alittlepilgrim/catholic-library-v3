# Reflections and Questions – Chapter V. After the Grace of Christmas

## Reflection 1

During her first communion retreat, Thérèse came to the sobering realization that she “was still in the _swaddling clothes of a child_” (S 152)! “In the morning, I found it very nice to see all the students getting up so early and doing the same as they; but I was not yet accustomed to taking care of myself. _Marie_ was not there to comb and _curl_ my hair, and so I was obliged to go and timidly offer my comb to the mistress in charge of the dressing rooms. She laughed at seeing a big girl of eleven not knowing how to take care of herself... . During my retreat I became aware that I was really a child who was fondled and cared for like few other children on earth, especially among those deprived of their mothers” (S 118).

This realization that she had remained a child began to have its effects upon Thérèse; she tried to do household tasks. “I wasn’t accustomed to doing things myself. Céline tidied up the room in which we slept, and I myself didn’t do any housework whatsoever.

After Marie’s entrance into Carmel, it sometimes happened that I tried to make up the bed to please God, or else in the evening, when Céline was away, I’d bring in her plants. It was for _God alone_ I was doing these things and should not have expected any _thanks_ from creatures. Alas, it was just the opposite. If Céline was unfortunate enough not to seem happy or surprised because of these little services, I became unhappy and proved it by my tears” (S 151).

Thérèse was crestfallen because she was _expecting_ Céline to make a big deal out of her small services. Thérèse thought that she was doing her little services for _God alone_, but her emotional reaction when the laudatory response that she expected from Céline was not forthcoming, told her otherwise. This small incident from Thérèse’s life provides us with much food for thought.

Have you ever noticed that the world’s reactions to your good will, efforts, acts of charity, etc., often do not correspond to your judgment of their worth? Have you ever felt either hurt, angry, or deflated when you were not given what _you_ considered _due_ recognition? Have you ever shared your hurt feelings with a friend, and he or she responded by saying, “What did you expect?” This is frequently a _rhetorical_ question, but it would behoove us to take it as a real question worthy of consideration. What _am_ I expecting? Howdo I imagine, expect, hope, and even need people to respond to my acts of kindness?

Have you ever noticed that the world’s response does not cater to your imagination? Do you feel hurt when someone simply says, “Thank you,” for a service rendered? Do you often feel deflated by a person’s response because your expectation was inflated? To what extent do you feel underappreciated because you over expect? Pauline’s feelings were once hurt because she rendered a service to one of the nuns and did not receive thanks. Thérèse responded, “I assure you, I too experience the feelings you are speaking about. However, I don’t allow myself to be trapped by it, for I _expect no_ _reward_ at all on earth. I do everything for God, and in this way I can lose nothing” (LC 42) (italic added). _Experiencing_ momentary hurt is one thing, but becoming _trapped_ by hurt is another. Is not the hurt that broods and festers more than the result of an unfulfilled _expectation_; is it not often rooted in an unconscious _demand!_ Think of it for a moment. Hurt is anger. We feel angry because we believe that someone did not give us our due. We don’t feel hurt or slighted because we are not invited to a party but because we _expected_ to be invited; we took it for granted. “It is my due; did I not invite her to my party?”

A _quid pro quo_ or tit for tat approach to life drains the joy out of giving and is a seed bed for anger and festering resentments. Conversely, we experience the joy of giving when we give freely. Perhaps “God loves a cheerful giver” ([[2-corinthians-09#^2-corinthians-09-7|2 Cor 9:7]]) because the person who gives freely of himself or herself is cheerful. And God wants nothing more than for us to enter into his joy.

In the Jewish tradition, a _mitzvah_ is a good deed that is done without expecting anything in return. When we choose to do a _mitzvah_, we experience the truth of the old adage that virtue is its own reward and that every act of love is twice blest; it blesses the one who gives as well as the one who receives.After Jesus washed the feet of his disciples, he said to them: “What I have given you is an example... _blest_ will you be if you put them into practice” ([[john-13#^john-13-15|Jn 13:15]], 17). _Makarios_, the Greek word that is translated _blest_ refers to the happiness that is proper to God alone. It is as if Jesus is telling his disciples, if you want to experience the happiness of God who is Love, then you must love.

## Question 1

**_Questions._** When we are self absorbed, we create our own misery; but, when we love freely, we become blest. Thérèse experienced this two-fold truth at the time of her Christmas conversion. “I felt _charity_ enter into my soul, and the need to forget myself and to please others; since then I’ve always been happy... God was able in a very short time to extricate me from the very narrow circle in which I was turning without knowing how to come out” (S 153–56). “How sweet is the way of _love_... it leaves nothing but a humble and profound peace in the depths of the heart” (S 276). We all know the burden of self absorption and the relief of being freed from our encapsulated ego. Daily life affords many opportunities for selfless giving which is the greatest gift we can give to ourselves. Where do these opportunities exist in your life?

## Reflection 2

When social worker and journalist Jacob Riis, who dedicated his life to eradicating the evils of the slums of New York City, became discouraged, he would meditate on the work of a stonecutter. “When nothing seems to help, I go and look at a stonecutter hammering away at a rock perhaps a hundred times without as much as a crack showing in it. Yet at the hundred and first blow it will split in two, and I know it was not that blow that did it—but all that had gone before.”[^4] There is a spiritual truth contained in this image. So often our repeated efforts to overcome a fault or failing in ourselves seem futile. Then, one day, we wake up and discover that a change has taken place within us. “The time will come when the Lord will repay [us] all at once,” as St. Teresa of Ávila puts it.[^5]Thérèse tells us that for ten long years she tried and tried and tried to overcome her oversensitivity but to no avail. Then the “work I had been unable to do in ten years was done by Jesus in one instant, contenting himself with my _good will_ which was never lacking” (S 153). There is a paradoxical truth contained in this statement. Namely, that our efforts are absolutely useless in the process of transformation because that is the work of grace alone; yet, in some mysterious way, our effort is a _necessary_ component of growth. Like _The Parable of the Sower_, we are powerless to have the good seed fall upon us, but we can keep the soil of our souls receptive in anticipation of the time of God’s visitation.

Perseverance without seeing the results of one’s labors is disheartening. In such moments of discouragement we need to believe that if we are doing what we believe is God’s will, then in _God’s time_, our efforts will bear fruit. But the fruit may not be what we expect, intend, or even imagine, for God’s ways are not our ways, and what we often experience as failure, may be to our betterment. Let us take Jacob Riis as an example. Riis may never have seen the stone split in two, that is, he may never have seen the fruits of his labor, but maybe the fruit that was born to him was the deep peace of _not needing to see_ it. As T. S. Eliot writes, “And what you thought you came for/Is only a shell, a husk of meaning/From which the purpose breaks only when it is fulfilled/If at all. Either you had no purpose/Or the purpose is beyond the end you figured! And is altered in fulfillment.”[^6]

## Question 2

How many times have you struggled to overcome a fault in your life but have failed only to wake up one morning to discover that God had removed it? Or have you ever “worked on yourself” in trying to overcome a fault, only to discover that your main fault was that you were taking yourself too seriously? Is there an area of your life in which you have an inordinate need to see the fruit of your labors and become overly discouraged when you don’t?

## Reflection 3

In Arthur Miller’s play _Death of a Salesman_, Willy Loman, who is a failure as a traveling salesman is at odds with his son Biff. Biff likes to work with his hands and is happy working as a ranch-hand in the West. But he is restless because he knows that he is a disappointment to his father. Willy has communicated to Biff in many ways that he will never amount to anything unless he is a success in the business world. Biff is torn. He wants to do what makes him happy but he desperately needs his father’s approval. The play opens as Biff returns home from the West in a state of confusion, which is summed up in a conversation that he has with his brother Happy. **Happy**: “But I think if you just got started—I mean—is there any future for you out there?”

**Biff**: “I tell ya, Hap, I don’t know what the future is. I don’t know—what I’m _supposed_ to want” (Italic added).[^7] Biff is torn between what he wants to do with his life and what his father wants him to do. Biff doesn’t know what he is _supposed_ to want. Willy wants Biff to become the man that he never was. Willy feels that he is a failure because he never achieved his dream and is hoping that it will be actualized through his son. In Miller’s play, we see what happens to a child in this circumstance; he is torn between his wishes and those of his parent.

Louis could have easily placed Thérèse in a similar conflict. When she was contemplating entering Carmel at the age of fifteen, Pauline feared the impact that Thérèse’s dream would have upon their father; she feared “the great sorrow which our father would endure for Thérèse was the real ray of sunshine in his life.”[^8] Thérèse was conscious of the pain that fulfilling her dream would cause her father. She must have been torn. Would she enter Carmel now as she wished, or should she wait until she was twenty-one to avoid causing her father pain? After all, she was the “Benjamin” of the family, her father’s youngest on whom he doted. Louis Martin wassixty-three; he was recuperating from a recent stroke and was a man who had been stripped of those he had loved the most. His wife was dead; Pauline and Marie were in the convent, and Léonie would soon follow.

Because Thérèse deeply loved her father and knew that leaving him would cause him great suffering, she was very vulnerable to his wishes. Louis had intimated that Thérèse would _someday_ enter a convent; but he was not ready for her to leave him so soon. It must have been very tempting for him to try to persuade Thérèse to postpone her decision. Without expressing it in words, Louis could have very easily communicated to Thérèse how much he needed her. “Thérèse, please wait a little while; I need you right now. Give me some time to recover from my stroke and the shock of your sister’s departure.” But he did not try to persuade her but only voiced a reservation. “He didn’t say one word to turn me from my vocation, simply contenting himself with the statement that I was still very young to make such a serious decision” (S 164). Even when the bishop of Bayeux told Thérèse that he shared her father’s reservation, Louis did not press his advantage. “The bishop, believing he’d please Papa, tried to have me stay with him a few more years, and he was very _surprised_ and _edified_ at seeing him take my part” (S 174).

How easy it would have been for Louis to place Thérèse in a state of confusion as Willy had Biff. Louis could have done this by planting a seed of doubt into Thérèse’s mind regarding her motive for entering Carmel _now_. Louis might have said the following: “Thérèse, I know that you have a great desire to enter Carmel now. But you have just heard the bishop’s reservation about your age. As you know, his opinion is shared by the ecclesiastical superior of Carmel, your uncle Isidore, your sisters Marie and Pauline, and myself. Thérèse, I support your vocation, but maybe God is trying to tell you something. Is it possible that you are going from door to door in search of the answer that _you_ want?”

He did not try to persuade her. He supported his daughter’s dream because he had made a heart wrenching decision not to cling to her. In a deeply symbolic gesture, he allowed Thérèse her freedom to follow her dream. “Papa seemed to be rejoicing with what comes from a sacrifice already made... [In] a _symbolic_ action my dear king. .. [went] up to a low wall, pointed to some _little white flowers_... and plucking one of them, he gave it to me explaining the care with which God brought it into being and preserved it to that very day. While I listened I believed that I was hearing my own story... I accepted [the flower] as a relic and noticed that, in gathering it, Papa had pulled all its _roots_ without breaking them” (S 164). Thérèse’s identity was shaped by her father’s act of handing her over to herself, so to speak. Is it any wonder that she begins her autobiography by referring to herself as “_a little white flower_” (S 13)? There are many and varied questions that arise from this incident for our consideration.

## Question 3

Have you ever found yourself trying to influence or manipulate a person’s choice to fit your need? Have you ever been in a situation where you have had the power to exert undue influence over the life direction of another? If you are a parent, how do you differentiate offering your children advice and guidance from imposing your dreams upon them?
